Mosquito-proofing Storm Drains – Barcelona IDEAS case study   Location: Barcelona  The problem:  Urban environments are increasingly vulnerable to vector-borne diseases due to climate change; mosquitoes of the Aedes and Culex genera species thrive in conditions of rising temperature, humidity, and rainfall, and can transmit multiple illnesses, including yellow fever, Zika, chikungunya, West Nile and dengue viruses. These species rely on stagnant water for egg-laying and larval development, making urban sewage systems with standing water or poor drainage ideal grounds for breeding and proliferation.  This is a persistent issue in Barcelona. Despite continuous monitoring and control efforts, mosquito populations have continued to grow, with city authorities usually applying biocides to breeding sites to limit mosquito activity.  The measure:  1. Following outbreaks of dengue and West Nile fever, the Barcelona Public Health Agency (ASPB) began piloting an infrastructure modification programme to ‘mosquito-proof’ storm drains in a manner that prevents the accumulation of standing water.   The goal of this intervention was to assess whether such modifications could reduce the establishment of new breeding sites, curtail mosquito proliferation in existing ones, and decrease reliance on biocide treatments in drains with high mosquito activity.  Initial results showed lower mosquito larval activity, reduced drain flooding, and a reduced need for biocide application.  2. A subsequent study, the randomised controlled trial IDAlErt storm drAin randomiSed controlled trial (IDEAS), was undertaken in 2023 to strengthen the evidence base for widespread implementation. The trial investigates the effect of mosquito-proofing storm drains on on the abundance of the two key species, Ae. albopictus and Cx. pipiens.

This trial sought to remove confounding variables from earlier observational studies by adopting a randomised control protocol. Eligible storm drains were randomly assigned either to receive mosquito-proofing or to serve as controls. A 200-metre buffer zone was established around each drain to account for mosquito dispersal capacity and to minimise cross-contamination between test areas.   For the treated drains, honeycomb bricks were installed at the base to reinforce the structure, before a concrete layer was added to create a gradient. The lowest point of this gradient was connected to the discharge pipe, ensuring that water flowed out rather than pooled. This rapid, low-cost measure was designed to eliminate stagnant water in catchment drains, thus rendering them unsuitable for breeding.  Control drains received no intervention, including the use of larvicides. Data was subsequently collected over a five-month period, measuring combined adult mosquito counts for both species, as well as the presence of adults or larvae to assess breeding site activity. Adult mosquitoes were captured using aspirators, while water samples were taken to detect larvae. 

Outlook:

While the results of the trial have yet to be published, researchers expect that modifying urban drain infrastructure to prevent upstream proliferation of mosquitoes could reduce the long-term operational costs associated with surveillance and control, including the use of biocides.   They also suggest that mosquito-proofing storm drains will be most effective when coupled with strong sanitation practices, efficient sewage management, and comprehensive mosquito control actions.   Finally, they underscore the contextual nature of the trial – Barcelona’s unique urban design may influence results, and further multi-site trials will be needed to validate findings across different environments. 

Nevertheless, the design and implementation of this trial should provide evidence and practical guidance for decision-makers and urban planners considering large-scale storm drain modification as part of sustainable mosquito control efforts.
