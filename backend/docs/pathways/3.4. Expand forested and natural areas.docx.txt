3.4. Expand forested (and natural) areas 
 3.4.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change

The expansion of forested and natural areas is widely regarded as a crucial measure in responding to the pressures of climate change, as these areas provide a broad range of ecosystem services essential for human and ecological wellbeing. Through photosynthesis, vegetation plays a primary role in atmospheric regulation, absorbing carbon dioxide and releasing oxygen into the atmosphere. Forested and natural landscapes also support services including pest and disease regulation, water purification and groundwater storage, soil conservation, (as plant roots bind the soil and prevent erosion,) and the mitigation of disaster risks, including floods and droughts. They harbour genetic resources of potential medicinal value, provide habitats for countless species, (importantly, pollinator species), and offer aesthetic, spiritual and recreational benefits that contribute to human wellbeing. Forests also offer raw materials, such as timber, fibres, and resin, and thus underpin multiple livelihoods and economies connected with forestry. Finally, forested and natural environments are also capable of cooling local temperatures and improving air quality, filtering pollutants such as nitrogen, sulphur and other chemicals.   As climate change poses multiple threats to human societies, exacerbating water scarcity and soil infertility, fuelling temperature increases, and amplifying the frequency and severity of extreme weather events, the expansion of forested and natural areas is considered an effective adaptation measure, helping to sustain the provision of essential ecosystem services and increasing overall resilience to climate impacts.    However, climate change equally endangers forested and natural landscapes; in particular, rising temperatures, the salinisation and acidification of freshwater sources, and the increasing occurrence of disturbances, such as windthrow, droughts, floods, and wildfires, accelerate the degradation or loss of forests and natural habitats.  Climate change-induced species invasions pose an additional threat, capable of eroding native biodiversity and destabilising natural ecosystems.   Indeed, the health of forested areas and the climate crisis are intrinsically linked: as climate change accelerates the destruction of forests and natural areas through extreme weather and ecological disruption, the degradation and unsustainable exploitation of forests, are, in turn, significant drivers of climate change. Depending on their overall health, these areas either absorb atmospheric CO2 or release it; once compromised, these ecosystems risk shifting from functioning as a carbon sink, sequestering warming greenhouse gases, to becoming net carbon sources, exacerbating climate change.   Consequently, the expansion of forested and natural areas represents a critical adaptation tool – by reinforcing ecosystem integrity, these measures can help buffer losses within global forest ecosystems, reduce the severity of destructive events like flooding and droughts, regulate increasing temperatures and improve air quality, and safeguard the continued provision of essential ecosystem services. Afforestation, for example, can increase groundwater levels, strengthening resilience to prolonged droughts, and improve biodiversity conservation statuses, providing habitat for climate-threatened species.   Moreover, afforestation is also recognised as a potential form of carbon capture and storage, positioning it as both an adaptation and mitigation strategy. Increasing climate-induced stressors, including wildfires, windthrows, and pest outbreaks, risk eroding the capacity of forests to function as net carbon sinks. Expanding forests and natural areas, therefore, not only increases climate resilience, but is central to combatting this phenomenon and contributing to long-term carbon storage and climate stability.   The EU places a strong emphasis on this dual role of adaptation and mitigation in its policies. The EU Forest Strategy for 2030 seeks to protect, restore, and sustainably manage forests to combat climate change and biodiversity loss. Core actions include improved monitoring, targeted restoration efforts, financial incentives for forest owners, and, most relevant, the planting of three billion trees by 2030.

Complementing this strategy, the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF)  Regulation sets an EU-wide target to increase net greenhouse gas removals by approximately 15% by 2030, reversing current declining trends. This legislation encompasses emissions and removals linked to land-use conversions, such as afforestation on former cropland, grassland, wetlands, and other land categories, and introduces carbon farming initiatives to promote sustainable land management.   Finally, the EU Biodiversity Strategy sets out a commitment to plant at least 3 billion additional trees across the Union by 2030; these measures are to be carried out in respect of ecological principles, with the EU Forest Strategy specifying recommendations on “planting and growing the right tree in the right place and for the right purpose”.   At the national level, several EU member states have proclaimed their intention to incorporate forest expansion into their adaptation and mitigation strategies. In 2022, following a summer of devastating wildfires, the French president Emmanuel Macron announced his government’s intention to plant 1 billion trees – an equivalent of 10% of France’s forested land, over a 10 year period, to combat climate change and increase biodiversity. 

3.4.2. Adaptation measures 

	•	Afforestation on previously non-forested land: As specified in the LULUCF regulation, this would include afforestation of former cropland, grassland, wetlands, and settlements. 
	•	Urban and peri-urban forest expansion: Examples include expanding existing urban woodlands, unused municipal lands, developing new green belts, etc. 
	•	Increasing urban green and blue spaces (see 4.1. for further details)
	•	Agroforestry expansion on non-forested agricultural land: Agroforestry expansion is a sustainable land-use management practice that integrates trees and natural elements, such as hedges, into farmland, to enhance biodiversity, improve soil health, and increase farm productivity. (Further details can be found in 3.1. Sustainable farming strategies). 
	•	Shelterbelt expansion: Shelterbelts are barriers of trees, bushes and shrubs, designed to reduce wind speed and prevent soil erosion. These can be implemented in forested areas, wetland areas, coastal areas, etc. Coastal shelterbelts, in particular, can help maintain ecological balance, while preventing and mitigating flooding, storms, cyclones and tsunamis in coastal regions. 
	•	Mangrove expansion: Mangrove ecosystems are highly effective in storing carbon, supporting marine biodiversity, and fortifying coastlines against erosion and extreme weather events. 
	•	Riparian woodland or green buffer creation on non-vegetated banks: This measure involves the establishment of vegetation adjacent to streams and rivers, normally consisting of grass, shrubs, and trees. 
	•	High-altitude forest expansion: As climate zones shift and temperatures rise, areas that were previously too cold or inhospitable for tree growth may become suitable for afforestation. This includes higher elevations in mountainous regions and northern latitudes, such as parts of the Nordic countries. Expanding forests in these zones can provide multiple adaptation benefits, by reducing soil erosion and mitigating natural hazards such as landslides and avalanches, particularly in snowy high-altitude environments.
	•	Construction of ecological corridors/green networks: Corridors facilitate species migration and genetic exchange under changing climatic conditions, augmenting and enhancing the connectivity of safe habitats for wildlife. They largely consist of trees, bushes, and vegetated areas, and support biodiversity across fragmented landscapes.  


3.4.3. Co-benefit of providing healthy ecosystems

The expansion of forested and natural areas can offer the added co-benefit of supporting infectious disease resilience by contributing to biodiversity support and the overall health and stability of ecosystems.    Biodiversity and ecosystem health are both associated with greater resilience to infectious disease. In degraded ecosystems and areas of weakened genetic and species diversity, the complexity and heterogeneity of the natural system are compromised, which makes it more vulnerable and at a heightened risk of creating new opportunities for disease emergence. Notably, the risk of infectious zoonotic diseases is often linked to the degradation or destruction of ecosystems; research further specifies that the loss of pre-existing biodiversity, such as after deforestation, is strongly associated with substantial increases in infectious disease outcomes. Conversely, more biodiverse environments are widely understood to be more resistant to disease emergence due to the ‘dilution effect,’ a phenomenon in which diverse ecosystems can offer pathogens a greater variety of hosts, some of which are less competent reservoirs or less susceptible to disease. This reduces the likelihood of successful transmission, thereby diluting opportunities for the pathogen to thrive. Consequently, mitigating biodiversity and habitat loss by expanding forested and natural areas can help control the presence and spread of disease vectors, thereby reducing the risk of infectious disease. Constructing wetlands, for example, has been shown to help mitigate antimicrobial resistance (AMR) – a serious threat to public health as it can render standard treatments for infectious diseases ineffective – by limiting the dispersal of pathogens and antibiotic-resistant bacteria in riverine systems.   When expanding forested and natural areas, prioritising developments that enhance ecosystem connectivity can be particularly effective for supporting biodiversity and overall ecosystem health. Creating ecological corridors, greenbelts, or urban greenways reduces habitat fragmentation, linking forests, natural areas, hedgerows, trees, parks, and other green and blue spaces. In doing so, these connections provide continuous refuge and mobility for species, maximising biodiversity protection and support. This strengthens ecosystem health, which, in turn, helps to reduce the risk of infectious diseases. A New York study of vertebrate diversity and disease transmission illustrates this relationship, in which forest fragmentation was found to decrease the number of different vertebrate species, allowing white-footed mice – highly competent hosts for Lyme-disease-carrying ticks – to dominate, elevating the overall Lyme risk for nearby humans. Conversely, in more diverse forests, ticks were also diverted towards less competent hosts like squirrels and birds, which diluted opportunities for disease transmission. Supporting habitat connectivity is especially critical for maintaining pollinator diversity, which helps ensure reliable pollination and, in turn, sustains plant communities, wildlife, and overall ecosystem health.  
Consequently, expanding forested and natural areas can support biodiversity and ecosystem health, reducing the prevalence and transmission of certain infectious pathogens and enhancing overall resilience to infectious disease.   3.4.4. Co-benefit of safe and clean water resources

The expansion of forested and natural areas can offer the co-benefit for infectious disease risk of supporting the availability of safe and clean water resources.  Thriving ecosystems play a vital role in the water cycle, storing, filtering, and purifying water, regulating flow, protecting from drought, and mitigating floods, which otherwise increase the risks of contamination from storm runoff and sewage overflow. Biodiversity strongly influences water cycles in wetlands, streams and aquifers, through processes such as energy exchange, and evapotranspiration from trees and plants; indeed, research demonstrates that functional biodiversity in forests directly correlates with improved water regulation, helping to moderate flows, prevent floods and maintain water availability. Expanding forested and natural areas in ways that support biodiversity thus strengthens the provision of water-related ecosystem services.  Additionally, healthy forests and biodiverse environments are closely linked to water quality.  Forests and connected vegetation purify water by slowing runoff, trapping organic matter, and filtering sediments and pollutants through root systems. Marshes, riparian buffers, and aquatic plants have also been shown to absorb harmful compounds such as ammonia, nitrates, and phosphorus. This natural filtration contributes to overall water quality – in contrast, deforestation is associated with the loss of this ecosystem service, and reduced water quality. Indeed, higher forest densities near waterfalls have been shown to strengthen water purification.   Expanding forests and natural areas can therefore support the availability of safe and clean water resources; this is particularly evident when previous cropland or agricultural areas are converted to forests, as research shows that afforestation and the replacement of livestock grazing with forest cover can significantly improve water quality outcomes.  By enhancing biodiversity and supporting safe and clean water resources, forest and natural area expansion ultimately reduces infectious disease risk: safe, potable water is fundamental not only for human survival, but equally for essential hygiene practices that limit disease transmission. Maintaining the availability of clean water thus supports public health, preventing the consumption of low-quality water and reducing outbreaks of infectious waterborne diseases linked to contaminated sources.

Supporting this, areas with increased vegetation diversity have been shown to promote microbial communities that help break down contaminants, eliminating pathogens such as Escherichia coli (E. coli), a major cause of waterborne illnesses. Furthermore, forest restoration, particularly in riparian zones, has been shown to effectively protect water bodies from pollutants, increasing water quality, supporting safeguarding freshwater resources and reducing risks to human health. 

3.4.5. Co-benefit of enhanced mental health & physical activity

Expanding forested and natural areas can play a meaningful role in improving mental and physical health, and, in turn, lowering both individual and collective vulnerability to infectious disease risks.   Natural environments and green and blue spaces are widely recognised for their capacity to offer considerable health and wellbeing benefits – living near, spending recreational time in, and feeling psychologically connected to nature are consistently associated with improved mental health outcomes.   These benefits are thought to arise through mechanisms such as increased social interaction, cognitive restoration, reduced stress, and psychological recovery. Research has shown that densely urbanised areas with limited access to natural spaces are linked to higher risks of psychosis and depression in both women and men. Greener cities have been associated with long-term improvements in mental health, highlighting the potential of urban afforestation and greening policies to deliver sustained public health gains, and blue spaces, whether oceans, beaches, lakes or rivers, are also increasingly recognised for their wellbeing benefits, with both visual exposure and physical visits shown to positively influence mental health. Nature connectedness more broadly is positively associated with wellbeing and negatively associated with psychological distress – visiting natural areas has even been linked to a reduced likelihood of using medication for depression.   Where expanding forested and green areas increases the accessibility of natural spaces and provides opportunities for connection with nature, these measures thus offer additional health and wellbeing benefits.   In urban and peri-urban areas, green and blue spaces can equally contribute to a more tranquil environment by buffering noise – tree and plants are particularly efficient at doing so, and research shows that hedges, green walls, and green roofs exhibit strong potential for sound insulation. Chronic or sudden exposure to noise, particularly from traffic, can adversely impact the central nervous system, increasing the susceptibility to mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, suicide, and increasing the risks of cardiological problems and stroke, impaired cognitive performance and even behavioural problems in both children and adolescents. Implementing green and blue infrastructure to reduce and distance noise can therefore meaningfully improve mental and emotional wellbeing, both indoors and outdoors. 
The same applies to heat management, as trees, vegetation and natural environments help cool temperatures, providing relief and reducing both immediate and long-term health risks associated with prolonged heat exposure (see 3.4.6. Co-benefit of heat management). Cities can be 10-15°C hotter than surrounding rural areas, a growing problem that can be mitigated by greening urban spaces.   Furthermore, forests and natural environments typically offer cleaner, purified air, reduced traffic, and open areas, providing opportunities for outdoor recreation and physical activity, both of which are strongly linked to improved health and wellbeing and reduced rates of depression. In particular, spending purposeful time engaging all five senses in forested areas – a practice known as ‘forest bathing’ (or Shinrin-yoku in Japanese) – has been shown reduce stress hormone levels, lower blood pressure and heart rate, and increase serotonin and vigour, demonstrating significant positive effects on mental health, especially in individuals with depressive tendencies. Forest bathing has also been associated with decreased blood glucose levels in patients with type 2 diabetes and may have preventive effects on cancer by increasing anticancer proteins in human cells.    The health benefits of increased contact with forest ecosystems and natural areas may even extend to long-term effects associated with human exposure to greater microbial diversity. For instance, research shows that the risk of developing asthma is lower in children living on traditional farmland, an environment rich in microbial variety. This has led to the hypothesis that similar benefits may result from exposure to the diverse microbial communities present in biodiverse natural environments, as these microbes can transfer from soil and air to the human gut, supporting a range of health and wellbeing outcomes. 
 Broadly speaking, natural areas provide opportunities for recreation, exercise, and learning, as well as nurturing a sense of place and aesthetic value, all of which can decisively contribute to better health and wellbeing. As climate change is projected to worsen physical and mental outcomes worldwide – due to heat stress, air pollution, increased infectious disease risk, pressure on health systems, and by extreme weather events causing PTSD, anxiety, and depression – particularly among vulnerable individuals and communities, expanding forested and natural areas represents a meaningful and effective strategy to mitigate the growing burden on physical health, mental health and overall wellbeing. 

In supporting mental and physical health and promoting physical activity, these measures offer key benefits for strengthening resilience against infectious diseases. As evidenced, access to healthy natural environments is consistently linked to reduced stress, anxiety, and depression; by buffering environmental stressors like noise and pollution they also enhance psychological, physical, and respiratory health. Expanding forested areas not only builds resilience against life-threatening events like flooding, but also encourages outdoor activities and regular physical exercise, which are associated with improved immune response, mental health, and cardiovascular function, among other benefits. 
 Ultimately, the varied health and wellbeing benefits offered by forested and natural areas are closely linked to stronger immune function, which directly reduces individual vulnerability to infectious disease. Sudden or chronic stress and mental health conditions can impair immune function, reducing the body’s ability to combat pathogens and potentially leading to more severe disease outcomes; chronic illnesses such as cardiovascular and respiratory diseases also compromise immune resilience.   Consequently, expanding forested and natural areas that enhance access to nature and amplify their health benefits can offer important improvements for both mental and physical health, thereby lowering both individual and collective vulnerability to infectious disease risks. 


3.4.6. Co-benefit of heat management

Expanding forested and natural areas, where such expansions improve the quantity and accessibility of cooler green spaces for human populations, can offer the important co-benefit for infectious disease risk of heat management. 
 Increasing forest cover, natural environments, and green and blue spaces is an effective strategy for heat management, as these areas can produce local cooling effects that help regions better cope with elevated temperatures and heatwaves. It is universally recognised that rural and natural areas are typically cooler than urban centres – often by as much 15°C – which is why many seek refuge in these areas during summer holidays or periods of extreme heat. This cooling occurs because vegetation limits local heat stress by reducing solar radiation through shading, and by lowering surrounding temperature via heat absorption and the release of water vapour through leaves (transpiration) and from the soil (evaporation). While the highest cooling effects (both in terms of spatial extent and intensity,) are observed in large natural spaces such as urban parks, the effect is also produced by smaller vegetated areas, including ground cover and street trees. 

Expanding forested and natural areas in urban settings, in particular, can thus deliver localised cooling benefits that help mitigate extreme temperatures, lessening the severity of heatwaves in cities and reducing the public health impacts of excessive heat (see also 4.1.1.1 Heat Management for further details). By decreasing the urban heat island effect, these spaces can lower the incidence of heat-related illnesses, including heat exhaustion, heatstroke, and dehydration, while also alleviating indirect detrimental impacts of heat on mental health, and cardiovascular and respiratory systems. In worsening these illnesses, heat renders individuals more susceptible to infections, increasing overall vulnerability to infectious diseases – heat-related illnesses are equally associated with an increase in mortality, thus reducing overall public health resilience.  Consequently, by mitigating extreme temperatures, the expansion of forested and natural areas can both directly reduce the incidence of heat-related illnesses and thus vulnerability to infectious disease, and indirectly help preserve healthcare system capacity, ensuring better responsiveness during infectious disease outbreaks, as fewer resources are diverted to treat preventable, heat-aggravated conditions.  
The cooling effects of natural environments may also reduce broader infectious disease risks associated with higher temperatures: heat acts as a catalyst for the transmission of several infectious diseases and is particularly capable of accelerating the expansion of vector-borne diseases. Many species of mosquito, for example, responsible for transmitting dengue, chikungunya, malaria, yellow fever and West Nile virus thrive in warmer conditions. Dengue-transmitting species, for instance, have been shown to accelerate their larval development, biting frequency, and viral replication at higher temperatures, (a factor that also enables the expansion of their geographic range into higher altitudes and previously cooler regions).   Additionally, expanding forested and natural areas in urban environments increases vegetation cover – an equally important factor in mitigating the urban heat island effect on infectious disease hazards. Research indicates that urban areas with low vegetation cover and heat-retaining surfaces, (such as dark roofs and concrete roads), are associated with significantly higher dengue incidence, whereas those with high vegetation cover have been shown to experience approximately 95% lower dengue incidence rates, where land surface temperature averaged 29°C.    Beyond vector-borne risks, extreme heat can also compromise food safety. High air temperatures can adversely affect food quality during transport, storage and handling, particularly if heatwaves cause power outages or disruption of electrical, refrigeration and cooking systems; these factors can increase the likelihood of foodborne illnesses, particularly during summer months. Helping to cool urban environments by expanding green and natural areas may thus indirectly reduce the risk of such outbreaks.  Finally, extreme heat also affects health indirectly by contributing to poor air quality and limiting people's ability to work or exercise outdoors. Reduced physical activity and prolonged indoor confinement can negatively impact overall health, weakening individual resilience to infectious diseases. By mitigating extreme heat, forested and natural areas can help preserve air quality and support opportunities for outdoor work and physical activity, thereby contributing to better health and stronger resistance to infectious disease. 

Of course, while expanding the quantity of urban forested and natural areas plays a key role in mitigating the urban heat island effect and generating co-benefits for the health of local populations, the accessibility of these environments, both rural and urban, is important to consider; beyond their cooling effects, these spaces provide effective refuge from extreme heat, and opportunities for physical activity and recreation only if readily accessible to the public. Therefore, alongside efforts to increase forest cover and natural environments, ensuring equitable access to these spaces, (where safe and appropriate) should also be pursued.  

Building on these considerations, research suggests that forest expansion in Europe could contribute to regional cooling: European forests have shown strong daytime cooling effects during hot seasons and heatwaves, functioning as natural refuges thanks to their high evapotranspiration potential under elevated temperatures. However, it should be observed that in colder Northeastern European regions, forest cover has been found in some cases to cause slight increases in land surface temperature. Thus, while expanding forested and natural areas remains an appropriate climate adaptation measure for managing heat and mitigating heat-related health impacts, its effectiveness may vary depending on geographic location and season. 
 Ultimately, expanding forested and natural areas, particularly in urban environments, has the potential to reduce both the drivers and consequences of infectious disease risk. By alleviating heat stress, these spaces may not only limit the environmental conditions that facilitate disease emergence and transmission, but also reduce competing demands on healthcare services, maintaining their ability to respond effectively to infectious threats. In this way, the heat management potential of expanding forested and natural areas offers benefits for addressing the intersecting infectious disease risks intensified by rising and extreme temperatures.   



3.4.7. Trade-off of ecological disruption  

Expanding forested and natural areas, if implemented without consideration for broader ecosystem health, can pose the trade-off for infectious disease risk of disrupting ecosystems.   Afforestation, tree planting, and natural area expansion projects may disrupt ecosystems, particularly when interventions prioritise carbon sequestration above all else, are implemented in unsuitable locations, involve inappropriate species, or fail to consider wider impacts on biodiversity, water resources, and wildfire risk. For example, research indicates that many ecosystems which do not naturally support dense tree cover are targeted for large-scale tree planting. Establishing tree plantations in areas historically devoid of forests can destroy the habitats of plants and animals adapted to open ecosystems, (in addition to threatening the livelihoods of people dependent on those ecosystems to produce wild game and domestic livestock).   Additionally, while some expansion projects prioritise climate-resilient and biodiversity-enhancing tree species, many afforestation projects plant large areas with a single, fast-growing species of tree selected for carbon sequestration, a practice known as ‘monoculture afforestation’. These monocultures can create ecological zones that support considerably less biodiversity than naturally regenerating forests. Moreover, widespread planting of non-native trees can alter the provision of ecosystem services, potentially reducing soil fertility, increasing erosion, and outcompeting native species, with collectively negative consequences for local biodiversity. 

Furthermore, tree planting in ecologically unsuitable areas can alter hydrological cycles; trees consume significant quantities of water, potentially drying out ecosystems, reducing groundwater recharge and decreasing stream flow, particularly in arid and semi-arid areas where trees are not the norm. Although trees can facilitate water infiltration into soils, they also increase water loss through evaporation of intercepted rainfall and transpiration from leaf surfaces – this renders the net effect of afforestation on water balance a complex issue that requires careful, site-specific evaluation. Consequently, where expansion projects alter the water cycle and reduce available water, they may negatively affect water-dependent flora and fauna. 

Afforestation can also increase the risk and severity of wildfires; widely planted species such as pine and eucalyptus are extremely flammable, and their proliferation can significantly raise wildfire risk, increasing the threat to surrounding ecosystems.    These forms of ecological disruption have direct implications for disease resilience. Biodiverse and healthy ecosystems provide a range of habitats and promote genetic variability, which can create natural checks on the spread of pathogens; variability in natural systems buffers against disease transmission by preventing the dominance of single disease vectors or hosts. When biodiversity is lost, this resilience weakens. In fact, ecosystem degradation and biodiversity loss have been identified as the primary environmental driver of infectious diseases. Therefore, forested and natural area expansion efforts that compromise overall ecosystem health may inadvertently increase vulnerability to disease outbreaks.  
Even when afforestation and expansion initiatives are designed to support biodiversity, they may still unintentionally increase disease risk by disrupting ecosystem balance.
This is because biodiverse environments may help suppress certain pathogens, yet they may, at times, favour their abundance. Indeed, the scientific community has long debated whether biodiversity mitigates or amplifies infectious disease risk, as vector-borne pathogens, generalist wildlife, and zoonotic pathogens are particularly sensitive to biodiversity changes. Specifically, studies evidence mixed relationships between biodiversity and disease, often dependent on scale: at large scales, biodiversity correlates positively with disease burden, but this association weakens over time; at smaller scales biodiversity tends to reduce disease risk, a phenomenon known as the ‘dilution effect’ (as discussed in 3.4.3. Co-benefit of healthy ecosystems). Ultimately, research asserts that biodiversity and habitat support can indeed reduce zoonotic disease spillover risk, but in some cases, targeted species or habitat management and biomedical interventions may be more effective. 

Consequently, even when designed to enhance biodiversity and support ecosystem services, forest and natural area expansion projects that are implemented without consideration of infectious disease risks, or the integration of appropriate disease control measures, can disrupt existing ecological balances, inadvertently increasing, rather than reducing, the likelihood of infectious disease emergence and prevalence, thereby posing risks to both human and wildlife health. 


 
3.4.8. Trade-off of increased human presence in natural areas

The expansion of forests and natural areas may result, intentionally or otherwise, in increased human activity in a given natural area. Such activity can disturb wildlife and heighten human-wildlife interactions, potentially escalating disease transmission and thus presenting a trade-off for infectious disease risk.    Natural environments harbour both pathogens and their vectors, spanning numerous vertebrates, invertebrates and even bird species, that can transmit infectious diseases to humans. For example, birds can serve as hosts for West Nile virus, avian influenza, and tick-borne pathogens. If a natural area lies along the flight path of migratory birds, (often a deliberate feature in biodiversity-focused or habitat expansion projects), the risk of zoonotic disease transmission may be heightened, as migratory birds are known to carry particularly high pathogen loads. Small mammals too, such as voles and mice, are carriers of hantavirus and leptospirosis, and serve as key hosts for tick-borne diseases. Invertebrates, most importantly, mosquitoes, ticks, and flies, act as primary vectors of illnesses including Lyme disease, tick-borne encephalitis, malaria, leishmaniasis, dengue, chikungunya, and Zika virus, all of which pose significant health risks to humans.   It is widely accepted that natural areas tend to harbour higher concentrations of wildlife and, consequently, greater densities of disease vectors – mosquitoes, for instance, are well documented to exhibit greater abundance and species richness in rural and natural areas compared to urban environments. Consequently, where forested and natural area expansion leads to increased human presence in these spaces, opportunities for disease transmission, and overall exposure to infectious pathogens are likely to rise.   To mitigate this trade-off, implementing disease monitoring measures alongside awareness campaigns can effectively reduce these risks; clear health warnings and educational materials, for instance, should be prominently displayed near natural sites to ensure that visitors take appropriate precautions against (geographically relevant) diseases associated with increased wildlife interactions. 



3.4.9. Trade-off of land use conflict 

Forest and natural area expansion can prove contentious, as these projects may not guarantee benefits for local livelihoods or address social justice concerns. When projects conflict with alternative land uses, this may exacerbate social and economic tensions, thereby heightening vulnerability to infectious diseases.  Significant land use conflicts can arise when large areas, set aside for afforestation or the development of natural ecosystems, compete with alternative economic uses, such as agriculture or urban expansion. During periods of financial and social hardship – marked by unemployment, housing crises, food shortages, concerns over farmers’ rights, and other broader pressures associated with economic stagnation – the tension between extractivist or growth-oriented development and ecosystem restoration and expansion can fuel social discord. 

This is clearly illustrated by the debate over England’s Green Belt, where the need to build new homes to alleviate a severe housing crisis clashes with efforts to preserve rural areas. The conflict is vividly encapsulated in Prime Minister Keir Starmer’s assertion that ‘homes must have a higher priority than nature and the environment’. 

A further example arose in September 2021 in Marseille, France, where a coalition of agrarian (and environmental) advocates protested the International Union for the Conservation of Nature’s (IUCN) move to raise the global protected-area target from 16% (at the time) to 30% by 2030, asserting, in part, that such ambitious targets would require conversion of traditional crop- or farmland and thereby adversely affect local agricultural communities.   Notoriously, the expansion of natural ecosystems can even conflict with the alternative environmental cause of Net-Zero emissions, as the restoration of land likely impedes its use for solar and wind energy installations. 

In terms of disease, land-use conflict tends to produce divisions and economic tensions, which can fuel social unrest and associated mental health concerns among affected populations; these factors carry consequences for infectious disease risk. Political protests, for instance, can increase exposure to disease by creating crowding at public demonstrations – these conditions increase the risk of transmission, as population density facilitates the spread of gastrointestinal and respiratory infections. Moreover, social unrest can trigger or exacerbate stress, anxiety, depression and other mental health conditions. These impacts are not only harmful in themselves but also reduce human resilience to disease, as poor mental health can impair immune function, reducing the body’s ability to combat pathogens and potentially leading to more severe disease outcomes.

An illustrative example of this trade-off relates to rural hospital provision. In certain European regions, rural hospitals are sparse; constructing a major healthcare facility in a natural area would bolster public health, potentially reducing vulnerability to infectious disease, yet plans for construction could conflict with afforestation projects to improve an area’s environmental status. Similarly, road infrastructure that improves access to hospitals and pharmacies could equally conflict with nature development projects. 

Furthermore, if the imposition of protected areas curtails job opportunities, such as through reduced crop production, this can diminish income for certain communities; in accordance with a One Health understanding, economic hardship and poverty is often associated with an increase in vulnerability to infectious disease.  

Consequently, while forested and natural area expansion remain effective measures for climate adaptation, where they generate or intensify land-use conflict that leads to both direct exposure risks and associated mental and physical health challenges, they carry a trade-off for infectious disease risk.    To mitigate this trade-off, participatory and inclusive strategies, such as those that integrate local stakeholders, are considered effective. By incorporating diverse perspectives and expertise during the planning and implementation of conservation and restoration projects, these approaches can alleviate tensions and help ensure that outcomes align with broader aims of climate justice and the reduction of socioeconomic inequalities. Furthermore, in the context of afforestation, EU carbon farming schemes, (integrated into the Common Agricultural Policy’s eco-schemes,) show promise in balancing this trade-off by incentivising farmers to plant and maintain trees while preserving their capacity for crop production.  
3.4.10. Mitigation Potential 

Expanding forested and natural areas can also offer significant potential as a climate mitigation strategy. In many cases, climate mitigation is not just a co-benefit, but the primary goal of afforestation and forest expansion initiatives.  This is because forests and natural areas can reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) concentrations by capturing and storing carbon, mostly in the soil, with smaller amounts in trees and plants. (The amount of carbon stored depends on factors like tree age, light availability, and carbon present in the air, with younger trees demonstrating a tendency to absorb higher amounts). Consequently, afforestation and natural area expansion can potentially increase carbon sequestration, thus contributing to lower atmospheric GHG concentrations. 

This is critically important, as climate change is primarily caused by the accumulation of greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide and methane, in the atmosphere, which trap heat and disrupt global climate systems. Absorbing these gases slows the pace of global warming, helping to prevent the most severe impacts on ecosystems, human health, and economies. Therefore, decreasing greenhouse gas concentrations – whether directly through reductions, or indirectly through absorption – is the fundamental strategy for mitigating climate change.

The link between forest cover and carbon sequestration is well established. In Europe, forest carbon sinks have historically removed up to 430 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent annually. However, this capacity was reported to have declined by nearly one-third between 2010 and 2020 due to climate impacts, increased harvesting, and the natural ageing of forests. Despite this decline, the EU places emphasis on tree planting, afforestation, and sustainable forest management as important contributors to climate mitigation strategies. Indeed, under EU climate legislation, the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) sector is mandated to contribute to the Union’s emission reduction targets, with GHG removals from forestry formally integrated into the 2030 climate and energy framework (as detailed in 3.4.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change).   As outlined in 3.3.9. Mitigation Potential, biodiversity is also of vital important for carbon sequestration, as ecosystems with greater plant and species diversity tend to be more productive, sequestering and storing carbon more effectively. Nonetheless, many afforestation projects, particularly those focused singularly on carbon sequestration, often neglect biodiversity, prioritising a quantity-over-quality approach and favouring monocultures of fast-growing, non-native species to maximise carbon uptake over short timescales. While these species can increase sequestration by up to 25% compared to native types, such strategies can significantly disrupt local ecosystems.   As detailed in 3.4.6. Trade-off of disrupting ecosystems, these biodiversity-poor afforestation projects risk degrading habitats, decreasing biodiversity, and weakening ecosystem productivity, ultimately undermining their own carbon mitigation objectives. Biodiverse forests not only support broader ecological health but are themselves more stable and effective carbon sinks; it is therefore essential that carbon-focused afforestation projects minimise biodiversity trade-offs, to prevent mitigation goals being compromised by ecological disruption. 

Finally, and most concerningly, forests and natural areas face increasing threats from wildfires, which can transform reliable carbon sinks into major carbon sources – as forests burn, the carbon stored over decades is rapidly released, undermining carbon offset schemes and potentially counteracting the mitigation benefits of afforestation.  Consequently, careful site selection for afforestation projects is essential to avoid high fire-risk areas where possible, as is the integration of fire prevention and management strategies into afforestation planning.
