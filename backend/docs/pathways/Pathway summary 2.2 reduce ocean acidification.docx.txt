Summary – Pathway 2.2: Reduce ocean acidification
2.2.1 Mitigation & adaptation measures
As the concentration of CO2 in the Earth’s atmosphere is rising due to continued greenhouse gas emissions (GHG) from human activity, this also has profound implications for marine ecosystems. Between 25-30% of CO2 that is emitted into the atmosphere is absorbed by oceans where it reacts with seawater to form carbonic acid. This in turn lowers the pH-value of the water, leading to the progressive acidification of the oceans. It is estimated that global average ocean surface pH has already dropped by 0.1 since the pre-industrial era, a rate of change not seen in at least 55 million years. Around 50% of analysed marine animal species have been shown to be impacted negatively by ocean acidification, with the strongest effects exhibited by corals, echinoderms, and molluscs. Higher water acidity reduces the concentration of calcium carbonate in the water and thus inhibit the ability of corals and shellfish to build calcified structures. At the same time, the increased CO2-levels benefit photosynthetic algae and can lead to harmful algae blooms that disrupt entire ecosystems and large die-offs of marine animals. Ocean acidification thus harms both the marine environments as well as economic sectors and livelihoods that depends on these, such as fisheries and tourism.
Climate change mitigation is the main tool to combat ocean acidification but there also exist a number of viable adaptation strategies. Reducing anthropogenic CO2 emission is the priority in mitigating against ocean acidification. Projections estimate that ocean surface pH could fall from currently 8.07 to 7.67 by 2100 in a non-mitigating emissions scenario. If emissions peaked in 2030 and then reduced by 5% annually, this drop could be limited to a pH of 7.96. Restoring and protecting natural carbon sinks, both at land and in water, can help further reduce the absorption of excess CO2 into the oceans. Creating marine carbon sinks to locally reduce acidity, for example by planting kelp or seagrass near seafood harvesting sites, can also serve as an adaptation strategy to build climate resilience of ecosystems and fisheries. The implementation of water treatment systems to correct the carbonate chemistry of seawater in oyster hatcheries has also proven effective in strengthening aquaculture.
2.2.2 Intended outcome as mitigation/adaptation to climate change
Most mitigation and adaptation strategies for reducing ocean acidification are not explicitly framed as such. Instead, they often address climate change and its impacts in a broader way by reducing GHG emissions or building resilience of coastal communities. Nonetheless, these measures are integral to limit the acidification of oceans. The intended outcomes are a strengthening of marine ecosystems and animal health, while also safeguarding fishery or tourism industries that rely on these. 
2.2.3 Co-benefits for infectious disease
2.2.3.1 Safe seafood supply
While the link between ocean acidification and seafood-borne intoxications in humans is well established, for example for paralytic shellfish poisoning (PSP) induced by harmful algae blooms, the relationship to infectious disease is less well understood. Certain pathogenic bacteria have been observed to thrive under higher acidity, most notably Vibrio spp. However, most scientific evidence relates to how ocean acidification facilitates Vibrio infections in molluscan shellfish with unclear implications for zoonotic transmission to humans. Nonetheless, studies have shown that Vibrio strains relevant for human infection risk in Europe, such as V. parahaemolyticus and V. vulnificus, are not only highly adaptable to changes in seawater acidity, but in fact experience higher growth rates at lower pH values. For V. parahaemolyticus, optimum conditions have been demonstrated at pH 7.96, slightly lower than current levels and in line with projections for the coming decades. Ocean acidification thus carries significant risks for increased infectious disease hazard for humans. 
Conversely, the outlined adaptation and mitigation measures – by reducing the acidification of oceans or enhancing local resilience of ecosystems and safety of seafood supply – offer potential for co-benefits in infectious disease prevention. This could help combat the spread of Vibrio in European coastal water, especially in the Baltic and Black Seas, reduce contamination of oysters and mussels, and lower the risk for Vibriosis outbreaks in Europe.
