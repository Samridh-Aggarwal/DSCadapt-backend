1.4. Awareness raising and information sharing  1.4.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change 1.4.2. Adaptation measures
1.4.3. Co-benefit of providing safe and clean water resources
  In the context of water management, educating the public is seen as pivotal for fostering successful, society-wide efforts to sustainably manage water resources.  Effective awareness campaigns and information sharing can deepen public understanding of both the value of water and the threats it faces.   Sharing information about the importance of conserving water, of reusing water safely, and preventing water contamination can empower stakeholders and communities to safeguard both water quality and quantity, helping to ensure the availability of safe and clean water resources. 
1.4.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change  Clean water is critical for nature and human health and wellbeing. It is also a key resource for many economic sectors. Over-exploitation of water resources and growing impacts of climate change are leading to increasing clean water scarcity in many parts of Europe. Simultaneously, pollution further stresses this limited resource. Efforts to conserve water, preserve and improve water quality, and enhance water ecosystems are thus a critical pillar of climate adaptation.   The educational status of governance structures, economic sectors, and the wider public is considered a key non-climatic factor in shaping our vulnerability to water-related risks under climate change. As such, educational initiatives, focused on raising awareness and sharing information, are essential adaptation measures for addressing water scarcity and safeguarding water quality.   Public awareness campaigns that highlight the importance of natural infrastructure networks, (such as large-scale wetlands, coastal dunes, rivers, and urban parks), are essential for helping people understand how these ecosystems improve water quality and mitigate flood risks. Without such awareness, these natural systems may not receive the prioritization and support needed for their protection and integration into water management strategies.    Compared to other barriers to climate change adaptation in water management – such  as technological challenges related to drought – obstacles to information sharing and awareness raising are relatively limited, making this adaptation strategy a practical and effective investment.  Consequently, several European water laws and policy frameworks emphasise, and in some cases require, public education, awareness raising, and information sharing as essential components for conserving and ensuring the continued availability of safe and clean water. The Water Framework Directive contains requirements for enhancing public participation in water management, and guidance measures focused on the need for public awareness about water quality and its importance. The Regulation on minimum requirements for water reuse, establishing minimum standards for reusing urban wastewater in agriculture, also mandates member states to develop information campaigns to promote the benefits of water reuse and ensure safe reclaimed water use, making relevant information accessible to the public online or through other means.   Awareness-raising measures are considered particularly important for climate change adaptation in the context of water management, as water-related impacts affect every citizen, unlike some other climate risks that may be limited to specific stakeholder groups. Education can stimulate change in consumption patterns and attitudes, helping to fuel sustainable water management practices.    1.4.2. Adaptation measures  - Public campaigns on water conservation  - Public campaigns on circular economy and safe water reuse - Public campaigns on leakage reduction  - Publicity for water conservation and reuse products and technologies - Publicity for low-water use products  - Awareness raising of water footprint of crops:  E.g. Multiple water studies inform advertisement, journalism and consumption patterns for animal and plant based milks  - Environmental certifications or labels on low-water or water saving foods and products: Some of these certifications are regulated, while other labels are self-promoted. This can be considered a controversial practice that is too narrowly focused on water volume used, rather than considering additional quality-related dimensions of water management. - Implementing policies that integrate water resource education into national school curricula  - Informational posters and leaflets in public spaces  - Mandatory water conservation measures: Enforcing water conservation measures, such as hosepipe bans, often has the added benefit of a general raised public awareness - Water quality information systems (Drinking, Bathing, etc.): Example: Spanish National Drinking Water Information System (SINAC) – this database provides information on the quality and characteristics of drinking water, detects and prevents risks of contaminated water, and facilitates water and health surveillance systems.  - Sector-specific water conservation and reuse information (e.g., agricultural businesses) - Warning systems for the state of water quality - Public awareness campaigns targeting bathers and water users in natural ecosystems: This is considered key to helping prevent the establishment of invasive non-native species in water ecosystems, which in turn can help maintain their safety and quality - Public awareness campaigns focusing on the importance of natural infrastructure networks (e.g. large-scale wetlands, coastal dunes, rivers, urban parks)  - Public awareness campaigns about favouring a). plant-based, b). seasonal, and c). regional food: This can help lower the demand for high water consumption crops, animal products and water intensive processes related to transport, refrigeration, irrigation and artificial growing conditions. - Public awareness campaigns on water conservation and clothing: Specific textiles are grown organically and less intensively, requiring less water. Textile production is also estimated to be responsible for about 20% of clean water pollution from dyeing and finishing products.  - Public awareness campaigns on best practices for domestic water appliances: For example, how best to use a dishwasher to ensure that it is effectively more resource-efficient than manual dishwashing - Water meters and digital consumption displays:  This renders water use more visible to the public, and has shown to decrease water consumption. Meters and consumption displays may even be subsidised or their installation legally mandated.   - Mandating companies to implement employee training on water conservation and reuse - World Water Day  - Citizen engagement through citizen science projects   Awareness raising and information sharing measures focused on hygiene:  - Water-consumption hygiene awareness campaigns  - Emergency water preparedness information: This preparedness information should educate the public about the importance of storing potable water correctly to ensure availability during a crisis.  
1.4.3. Co-benefit of providing safe and clean water resources

Water education plays an important role in shifting public attitudes and behaviours toward more sustainable water use. By raising awareness and sharing practical knowledge, populations are better equipped and incentivised to conserve water resources, to safely store and reuse water, and to protect water sources from contamination. These actions collectively help to maintain the availability of safe and clean water resources, which, in turn, reduces exposure to contaminated water, helping to limit the spread of infectious waterborne diseases.   Furthermore, public education on the importance of water conservation, availability, and quality often extends to broader messages about hygiene and sanitation. This information can drive behavioural change in everyday health practices, such as improved handwashing and safe domestic water storage. These equally reduce exposure to contaminated water and limit the risk of outbreaks of waterborne infectious diseases.
