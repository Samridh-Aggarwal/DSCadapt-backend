  4.2. Building standards, regulations and guidelines 4.2.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change  Adapting the built environment to a changing climate is essential – people increasingly spend most of their lives indoors. Buildings are also major contributors to emissions, pollution and waste; in the EU they account for roughly 40% of total energy use and 36% of energy-related greenhouse-gas emissions. At the same time, climate change is increasing the incidence in Europe of health hazards, created by extreme weather events, prolonged periods of high temperatures and drought, and even the expanded presence of climate-sensitive disease vectors such as mosquitoes; it is therefore critical that buildings be best equipped to protect occupants from these escalating risks.   Improvements in building design and construction can improve energy efficiency and reduce overall energy consumption. Tailored measures can also help moderate indoor temperatures, reducing cooling and heating demand and creating comfortable, healthier interior environments. Specific measures can improve water efficiency and prevent water stagnation in plumbing, helping to conserve safe and clean water resources. Climate-resilient building measures may also focus on protecting a building’s own energy-generating or energy-saving features, such as solar panels and insulation, from hail, wind and other extremes. Finally, naturalistic building design measures can create calm spaces that enhance occupants’ mental health by encouraging moments of connection with nature. For these reasons, environmental standards, regulations and guidelines for buildings are effective instruments of climate adaptation, translating climate risk into mandatory or recommended design and performance outcomes.   Consequently, the EU Strategy on Adaptation to Climate Change emphasises the need to address climate vulnerabilities in the built environment, underscoring the important role of buildings and urban planning in large-scale adaptation. It promotes the integration of climate-resilience criteria into building construction and renovation, including nature-based solutions, such as green roofs to limit the urban heat island effect. Within this framework, building standards, regulations and guidelines are practical levers that can raise minimum performance, steer design, incentivise sustainable construction and ensure that major renovations contribute to a national stock of low-emission, ‘future-proof’ buildings.   Several EU-level instruments are of relevance for this sector-wide adaptation. The Green Public Procurement Criteria for Office Building Design, Construction and Management, while voluntary, helps public authorities to ensure that buildings are created with higher environmental standards, as does Level, the EU framework for sustainable buildings, which provides common metrics for reporting and comparing building performance. Both feed into the Renovation Wave Strategy, which aims to refurbish 35 million buildings by 2030, while doubling the annual EU rate of energy renovations. This initiative aims to improve energy efficiency and heating and cooling standards in renovations, incorporating features such as smart technologies and the use of sustainable construction materials.   However, regarding obligatory regulations, the recently revised European Directive on Energy Performance of Buildings is the European Union's (EU) main legislative instrument promoting the improvement of the energy performance of buildings among the Member States of the EU. It supports and guides member states in their policy decisions, regarding the heating and cooling of buildings, and specifies that, member states, regarding new buildings, shall take the necessary measures to ensure that new buildings meet the minimum energy performance requirements.   Obligatory requirements sit in the revised Energy Performance of Buildings Directive, the Union’s main legislative tool for improving building energy performance. It requires Member States to ensure that new buildings meet minimum energy performance standards, and that all residential buildings must acquire at least an E rating by 2030 and a D by 2033. All buildings should achieve A or B rating by 2040, moving towards the ultimate objective of a zero-emission building stock by 2050. (For non-residential buildings, a different, phased-out trajectory is specified.)   The same Directive introduces zero-emission buildings, mandatory from 2028 for public bodies and from 2030 for all other new buildings (subject to limited exemptions), with a zero-emission building considered to be one that combines very high energy performance with zero on-site fossil-fuel emissions.  The directive also specifies that energy performance standards are not confined to the ‘building envelope’, but rather, they extend to passive and technical elements such as day-lighting, ventilation and shading that curb demand for heating, cooling and lighting while preserving thermal and visual comfort. Member States are also encouraged to introduce specific energy performance standards for the rental sector such that energy-poor households also benefit.  The Energy Efficiency Directive is a complimentary regulation that obliges energy distributors and retailers to meet cumulative end-use savings targets. Efficiency gains in district heating and cooling can count towards these targets, reinforcing the link between system-level and building-level adaptation.  Regarding the enforcement of building standards and regulations, implementation is largely local, with national or regional bodies translating European objectives into context-specific standards. For example, in Italy, energy-efficiency requirements are integrated into regional building regulations and monitored by local municipalities.   Finally, many contemporary EU guidelines and best practices for urban development and building design emphasise the utility of nature-based solutions and green and blue infrastructure for climate adaptation. Measures such as green roofs and walls can effectively regulate building temperature, reducing internal temperatures by up to 15 °C, improve energy efficiency of buildings, improve outdoor air quality, and even help preserve water quality by preventing stormwater runoff. In fact, research projects that under future climate conditions, green roofs in urban areas could reduce annual HVAC energy consumption by up to 65% by 2100, making the integration of green infrastructure into building design a potentially crucial adaptive measure for meeting energy efficiency and emission reduction targets. Further details on these strategies are provided in section 4.1. Increasing Green and Blue Spaces. 

Ultimately, both mandatory standards and voluntary guidelines play a central role in adapting buildings to climate change. While binding regulations set the minimum requirements needed to drive consistent, large-scale change, voluntary frameworks can offer flexibility and incentivise innovation, supporting the building sector to tailor solutions and even go beyond mere compliance. Together, these measures help to protect buildings and their occupants from extreme weather and other climate-related hazards, while simulatenously increasing their energy and water efficiency, thus expanding the climate resilience of the European built environment.

4.2.3. Co-benefit of improved indoor & outdoor air quality 
Standards, regulations and guidelines for sustainable and climate-resilient buildings can offer the co-benefit for infectious disease risk of improving indoor and outdoor air quality.  Indoor Air Quality   Ventilation of buildings is essential to ensure indoor–outdoor air exchange, preventing the stagnation and build-up of indoor pollutants and contaminants. Air conditioning systems that only regulate temperature, without incorporating ventilation, can even worsen indoor pollution levels, making proper air exchange essential for maintaining indoor air quality. However, air exchange often carries significant energy costs, as incoming outdoor air must typically be heated, cooled or dehumidified, and increased air flow can weaken the effects of internal climate control. Consequently, under current energy performance standards, highly efficient buildings generally rely on mechanical rather than conventional, ‘natural’ ventilation, i.e., opening doors and windows.   These systems can offer added benefits for indoor air quality: uncontrolled or ‘conventional’ window opening is unreliable and may fail to maintain hygiene air exchange rates, resulting in elevated CO2 concentrations, whereas mechanical ventilation can filter incoming air, and modulate air flow relative to the occupancy, temperature, humidity and pollutant levels. Mechanical ventilation can also be combined with measures such as optimised demand-controlled ventilation and heat recovery systems, capable of conserving energy while improving indoor air quality. Indeed, mechanical ventilation in energy-efficient buildings has been shown to lower concentrations of chemical pollutants and biological contaminants compared to conventional ventilation, including volatile organic compounds, CO2, radon and mould spores. This is, in part, attributed to higher air-exchange rates which remove pollutants more effectively, and better moisture control which keeps relative humidity below thresholds that could favour harmful fungal growth. Modern HVAC (Heating, Ventilation and Air Conditioning) systems can even be programmed to adjust ventilation rates based on detected pollutant concentration (and other control parameters), a requirement for certain units specified in the EU’s Directive on ecodesign requirements for ventilation units.   Furthermore, regular inspection and maintenance of heating, ventilation and air-conditioning systems, as specified in the EU Performance of Buildings Directive, is recommended, to ensure optimal performance from an environmental, safety and energy point of view – independent assessment, and maintenance of HVAC equipment can limit the build-up of contaminants, helping to curb the indoor spread of infectious agents and improving overall air quality.  
Outdoor Air Quality  Energy-efficient standards are also capable of easing pressure on urban air quality. The more buildings meet, or exceed, stringent efficiency requirements, the less energy is consumed for heating and cooling, cutting urban CO₂ and pollutant emissions at source. Given that the European building stock accounts for 40% of energy use and is an important contributor to urban air pollution, compliance with building standards (and voluntary measures exceeding these) offers an effective strategy for improving outdoor air quality. Climate-smart building policies can also provide financial incentives for the installation of onsite renewable energy generation on buildings, such as solar panels, further reducing building-related emissions associated with the combustion of fuels.   Finally, green building elements, such as green roofs, can improve air quality and reduce greenhouse gas concentrations by capturing and storing carbon, filtering pollutants, absorbing dust and gases and catching particles in the leaves of plants. Multiple studies have shown that green roofs can reduce CO, CO2, SO2, and NO2, with different roof types and plant species demonstrating varying capacities to remove air pollutants. Green roofs can help cool and insulate buildings, lowering energy demand and, in turn, reducing fossil fuel consumption and associated air pollution. Integrating green infrastructure into building design can therefore improve overall urban outdoor air quality and even contribute modestly to improving indoor air quality through measures such as green walls, which act as natural air filters and can increase oxygen levels. Further details are provided in section 4.1.1.2. Improved Indoor and Outdoor Air Quality.   Air quality and Infectious Disease Risk  Improving both indoor and outdoor air quality has important co-benefits for reducing infectious disease risk. Air quality is closely linked to human health, as pollution and other airborne contaminants and pathogens are known to aggravate a variety of respiratory conditions and diseases. Air pollution, particulate matter and contaminants such as radon can increase the risk of strokes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, and lower respiratory infections. (The interaction between air pollution and extreme heat is particularly harmful to those with pre-existing conditions). These conditions weaken overall resilience, increasing individual vulnerability to infectious diseases; cardiovascular and respiratory illnesses, for example, are known biological risk factors for vulnerability to vector-borne infections. 
 Furthermore, weak building standards and poor ventilation that fail to regulate indoor moisture can create humid conditions that promote mould growth, which fuels the development and exacerbation of respiratory diseases, such as asthma: these can significantly undermine overall resilience to infectious and respiratory illnesses.   Conversely, improving air quality can alleviate respiratory and cardiovascular stress, helping to reduce the prevalence and severity of these health conditions and thereby lowering human vulnerability to infectious disease.   Importantly, cleaner air can also lower the transmission rates of respiratory infections, both by decreasing the concentration of airborne particulates that act as carriers for pathogens, and by improving overall respiratory health and immune response. Consequently, improving indoor air quality is critical to protecting building occupants from the transmission of respiratory viruses and bacteria, particularly in enclosed, shared environments.  An example of an international building standard that addresses this transmission risk is ASHRAE Standard 241, Control of Infectious Aerosols, which establishes minimum requirements aimed at reducing the risk of disease transmission through exposure to airborne pathogens in buildings (whether new, existing or under major renovation). While it is a voluntary standard (unless adopted or referenced in legislation), it acknowledges that microscopic particles exhaled by individuals can carry disease-causing pathogens that, as seen during the Coronavirus pandemic, must be effectively filtered to prevent exposure among building occupants. Notably, its requirements largely focus on air system design and maintenance.  
Conclusively, climate-resilient and sustainable building standards, regulations and guidelines can play a vital role in reducing both exposure and overall vulnerability to infectious diseases by improving indoor and outdoor air quality. Through reduced emissions, minimised airborne pollutants and improved ventilation, these measures can create healthier environments that support resilience to infectious diseases and limit their transmission.    4.2.4. Co-benefit of safe and clean water resources  
Climate-resilient building standards, regulations and guidelines increasingly focus on water conservation and preventing water contamination. By specifying how buildings should conserve, reuse and manage water, these instruments help to preserve safe and clean water resources, thus reducing the risk of waterborne infectious diseases. Maintaining access to safe, potable water is vital for hygiene practices that reduce disease transmission, while keeping water supplies uncontaminated is critical for preventing outbreaks of waterborne illnesses.   Building standards may improve water efficiency by encouraging the adoption of smart technologies and design strategies that minimise both water consumption and waste, such as low flow taps, real-time flow monitoring and smart leak detection systems. Buildings may even include green elements that harvest rainwater and reuse it internally as greywater for flushing, thus helping preserve potable water reserves.   The Portuguese Energy Agency’s voluntary Water Rating for Buildings scheme, AQUA+®, exemplifies how voluntary water standards can incentivise water reuse while cutting energy costs and minimising waste. As of 2024, 700+ certifications have been issued, leading to an estimated 19 million litres of water savings, and the scheme is being considered for inclusion in the national strategy for building renovation.   Climate-resilient building standards may also incorporate automated plumbing systems, equipped with sensors and timed– these have been shown to prevent water stagnation in plumbing, and to be capable of minimising scale and biofilm growth. This is particularly critical for infectious disease risk, as indoor water stagnation can favour the development of chemical and microbiological contaminants that pose potential health risks to occupants and can increase the risk of waterborne disease outbreaks.  Scale and biofilm growth is also associated with creating a suitable environment for a variety of microbes by supplying nutrients and offering protection - the development of Legionella (responsible for pneumonia-type Legionnaires’ disease) for example, is associated with biofilm. Reducing biofilm and scale growth is therefore key to reducing the risk of waterborne infectious diseases.  
Furthermore, integrating green and blue infrastructure into buildings can support improvements in water quality and help prevent deterioration of water quality following extreme weather events, thereby contributing to continuous availability of safe and clean water resources. For example, green roofs can slow stormwater runoff and reduce pressure on water systems, helping to prevent overload and avoiding contamination of water sources, in addition to filtering and improving the quality of runoff, (see 4.1.1.3. for further details).   Smart buildings may even incorporate water quality monitoring, which can alert managers to the presence of pathogens, allowing for rapid intervention and prevention of disease outbreaks. By actively monitoring and addressing water quality, buildings can support the sustained availability of clean water for essential hygiene and public health, which in turn, helps to limit the spread of waterborne diseases.  Collectively, building standards, regulations and guidelines that incorporate water efficiency and conservation measures can reduce the risk of waterborne diseases, both by preventing the contamination of water supplies and conserving safe and clean water for essential hygiene practices that reduce vulnerability to infectious diseases.   4.2.5. Co-benefit of heat management 
 Climate-resilient building standards and regulations play a crucial role in managing heat effectively, which can significantly reduce vulnerability to infectious diseases. Excessive indoor heat not only contributes to immediate heat-related illnesses, but can also exacerbate chronic health conditions, increase inflammation and weaken the immune system, factors that increase susceptibility to infections and reduce public health resilience during outbreaks.   A range of strategies can be employed to climate-proof buildings against high temperatures, including design principles, material choices, and technological innovations.  Architectural features such as optimal building aspect ratios and strategic solar orientation can reduce heat gain by balancing internal volume with external surface exposure, and by minimising direct sunlight exposure and absorption. These passive design choices can improve indoor thermal comfort without increasing energy demand.  Building materials further influence indoor temperature regulation: buildings constructed with thick walls or high thermal resistance materials absorb and transfer less heat, and thus require less mechanical cooling. Additionally, the use of phase change materials (PCMs), which store and release large amounts of heat during liquid-solid transitions, can help regulate internal temperatures and lower cooling loads.
Surface treatments can also contribute meaningfully to heat management, such as white and lightly coloured roofs and external walls. Similarly to reflective coatings, these surfaces reduce heat absorption, thus preventing an increase in internal temperatures. This equally applies to cool roofs, designed to reflect more sunlight and absorb less solar energy, lowering indoor temperatures and thus reducing energy consumption.  Climate-resilient building standards equally underscore the potential of integrating technological solutions into the building envelope (external walls, windows, doors, ceilings, and foundations). For example, smart sensors and digital climate control systems can enable real-time thermal regulation, using precise adjustments of HVAC systems and shading panels. These systems help maintain more stable indoor temperatures and humidity levels, thus helping to prevent heat distress and associated health complications.   Green and blue infrastructure, such as green roofs, vertical gardens, and the strategic placement of trees for shading, also offer effective solutions for heat management. These interventions produce local outdoor cooling effects through increase evapotranspiration and improved airflow, helping urban areas to combat high temperatures and heatwaves. They can equally help retain indoor cooling, increasing the overall energy efficiency of buildings. (See 4.1.4. for further details on green infrastructure and heat management).
 Importantly, building HVAC systems can negatively affect temperatures at the street level, as traditional air conditioning systems expel heat into the street, aggravating the urban heat island effect. However, energy-efficient buildings, particularly those with optimised HVAC, can mitigate this through reduced overall demand, and by reducing the heat emitted from these systems. Research shows that, comprehensively improved building efficiency can offset some of the outdoor temperature increases expected from climate change, even improving ‘passive survivability’ during potential power outages, when compared to lower building efficiency grades.   These combined architectural, material and technological measures collectively contribute to more effective heat management, and the provision of safer, comfortable indoor climates. This is vital for reducing the health impacts of heat and extreme temperatures, helping to prevent heat-related illnesses such as heat exhaustion, heatstroke, and dehydration, while critically alleviating the detrimental impacts of heat on chronic conditions, on mental health, and on cardiovascular and respiratory systems. In worsening these illnesses, heat renders individuals more susceptible to infections, increasing overall vulnerability to infectious diseases – heat-related illnesses are equally associated with an increase in mortality, thus reducing overall public health resilience. Consequently, by promoting thermal comfort, building standards can directly reduce the incidence of heat-related illnesses and thus vulnerability to infectious disease, and indirectly help preserve healthcare system capacity, ensuring better responsiveness during infectious disease outbreaks, as fewer resources are diverted to treat preventable, heat-aggravated issues. 

To this end, building regulations in Europe increasingly mandate the requirement of air conditioning or effective thermal comfort in workplaces, public spaces, and rental housing, etc. These regulatory requirements are essential not only for protecting occupants from heat but also for enforcing the provision of indoor environments that minimise heat-related health risks, including those associated with infectious disease vulnerability. 
4.2.6. Mitigation Potential   Building standards, regulations and guidelines can equally be powerful tools for climate mitigation: buildings play a critical role, not only in adapting to changing climate conditions but also in reducing greenhouse gas emissions. The European Union has set ambitious targets to support this, mandating that by 2030, buildings’ greenhouse gas emissions must be cut by 60%, final energy consumption reduced by 14%, and energy use for heating and cooling decreased by 18% to meet the overall 55% emission reduction goal. This urgency stems from the fact that buildings are responsible for approximately 40% of the EU’s total energy consumption and 36% of its energy-related greenhouse gas emissions. Consequently, enhancing the energy efficiency of buildings, minimising their carbon footprint throughout their entire life cycle, and improving their sustainability is critically important for reducing national emissions and meeting climate mitigation goals. Incorporating circular economy principles into building renovations can also reduce emissions associated with materials production and building waste.  Climate change is primarily caused by the accumulation of greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide and methane, in the atmosphere, which trap heat and disrupt global climate systems. Reducing these emissions slows the pace of global warming, helping to prevent the most severe impacts on ecosystems, human health, and economies. Therefore, reducing greenhouse gas emissions is the fundamental strategy for mitigating climate change.   Collectively, energy efficiency measures, whether it be those embedded in building codes, retrofitting and insulation strategies, or the integration of green and blue infrastructure (which can help reduce cooling demand and, consequently, energy-related emissions) thus offer an effective, targeted approach to climate change mitigation.
