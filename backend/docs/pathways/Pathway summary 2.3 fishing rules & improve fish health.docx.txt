Summary – Pathway 2.3: Fishing rules & improve ƒfish health
2.3.1 Adaptation measures
Targeted fish-health measures for both wild stocks and aquaculture form an important and cost-effective component of global climate adaptation. As climate change increases sea and freshwater temperatures and leads to deoxygenation, acidification, and more frequent extreme events, it raises physiological stress, disease incidence and mortality in both wild and farmed fish. Improving fish health therefore strengthens ecological resilience, reduces production losses, and cushions livelihoods and food security against climate shocks. A range of adaptation measures can be subsumed under fishing rules and improving fish health: 
	•	Strengthened surveillance, diagnostics and early-warning systems: Routine monitoring of pathogens, harmful algal blooms, and environmental stressors (temperature, oxygen) lets managers and producers detect emerging risks and trigger rapid responses (targeted closures, welfare measures, movement controls). Integrated early-warning systems that link environmental and health data reduce outbreak magnitude and mortality. International guidance and FAO technical supplements explicitly recommend this surveillance approach for adaptation planning.
	•	Reduce cumulative stressors through precautionary fisheries maquanagement: Reducing fishing pressure on climate-stressed populations (lower total allowable catches (TACs), seasonal or area closures, gear restrictions) lowers physiological stress and improves individual and population immune function. Ecosystem-based fisheries management that removes or reduces concurrent pressures (bycatch, habitat damage, pollution) increases the capacity of stocks to withstand heatwaves and other climate impacts. EU analyses and policy reviews emphasize linking fisheries rules to resilience goals.
	•	Habitat protection and restoration (climate refugia): Nursery habitats (seagrass beds, salt marshes, riverine riparian zones) often provide cooler, oxygen-rich microhabitats and bolster juvenile survival. Protecting and restoring these habitats (via MPAs, Natura 2000, or targeted rehabilitation) reduces exposure to stressors and disease vectors and creates climate refugia for sensitive life stages. European environmental agencies and scientific syntheses recommend habitat-based measures as core adaptation tools.
	•	Aquaculture biosecurity and adaptive husbandry practices: In farmed systems, improving water quality management, biosecurity, vaccination programs where available, stocking density controls, and flexible siting (or use of land-based recirculating systems) reduces disease risk and heat stress losses. “Climate-smart aquaculture” strategies—combining diagnostics, vaccines, diversified polyculture and husbandry adjustments—are repeatedly identified in the literature as high-value adaptation measures.
	•	Genetic resilience (selective breeding with safeguards): Selective breeding for thermal tolerance and disease resistance can increase resilience in farmed stocks, but must be deployed with genetic management to avoid reducing genetic diversity or harming wild populations through escapes. Scientific reviews highlight potential benefits and risks; hence genetic approaches are an adaptation option when paired with robust containment and monitoring.
	•	Spatially and temporally adaptive management (dynamic measures): Using near-real-time environmental data (sea-surface temperature, oxygen, harmful-algal-bloom forecasts) to apply dynamic closures or shift effort away from stressed regions reduces mortality and disease spread. Dynamic spatial management is increasingly feasible with remote sensing and improved monitoring, and is cited in European policy analyses as an emerging best practice.
	•	Integrated modelling, risk assessment and decision support: Linking stock assessments, pathogen models and climate projections enables managers to anticipate vulnerabilities and apply precautionary buffers (adjusted TACs, contingency rules). FAO and EU projects recommend integrated risk frameworks as central to making fish-health measures effective under climate uncertainty. 
	•	Socio-economic measures: diversification, co-management and safety nets: Adaptation is not only biological: supporting fishers and producers to diversify incomes, shift to less vulnerable species or gears, and co-manage measures locally increases social resilience and reduces the risk of non-compliance. Guidance on NAPs and FAO supplements emphasize linking technical measures to social protections.
Within the EU, the Common Fisheries Policy (CFP) constitutes the central legal framework regarding fishing rules and fish health. The CFP governs catch limits (TACs), quota distribution, technical conservation measures and regionalisation of management across EU waters. It provides the principal lever for reducing fishing pressure on vulnerable stocks and for embedding ecosystem-based and adaptive management approaches that benefit fish health and climate resilience. EU technical reports and the Commission’s policy material have explicitly considered how the CFP can adapt to climate change and promote resilience. The CFP is underpinned by evidence from the International Council for the Exploration of the Sea (ICES) which provides the independent stock assessments, ecosystem advice and increasingly climate-aware analyses that inform TACs and management advice in the Northeast Atlantic and Baltic. Incorporating climate signals and pathogen risk into ICES advice is an ongoing technical and policy challenge being taken up in regional fora.
Furthermore, the Habitats Directive, Marine Strategy Framework Directive and Natura 2000/MPA networks create the EU’s legal instruments to protect critical habitats and nursery areas which are key components of habitat-based fish-health and adaptation strategies. Spatial planning and the expansion of MPAs can be used intentionally to create climate refugia for vulnerable species. 
At global multilateral level, technical frameworks for integrating fisheries and aquaculture into national adaptation planning are provided by the FAO guidelines and the UNFCCC National Adaptation Plan supplements. These cover surveillance, risk assessment, gender and livelihoods considerations and serve as the reference point for member states developing sectoral adaptation plans.
EU agencies and programmes (e.g., CINEA/EU studies, EU recovery and cohesion funds) are beginning to prioritise investments in sustainable fisheries, habitat restoration and climate-smart aquaculture. These financial tools can underwrite surveillance systems, on-farm upgrades and habitat measures. Recent EU analyses and news items show growing budgetary commitments to ocean protection and fisheries resilience.

2.3.2 Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change
The primary intended outcome of climate-responsive fishing rules, such as adaptive TACs, seasonal closures, and habitat protections, is to maintain sustainable fish populations and ecosystem stability under changing environmental conditions. By aligning catch limits and spatial measures with scientific advice that incorporates climate impacts, the aim is to prevent stock collapses, preserve spawning biomass, and maintain the ecological functions that underpin fisheries productivity. These rules are also designed to increase the resilience of fishing-dependent communities, ensuring that livelihoods and food supplies remain viable despite climate-driven shifts in species distribution and abundance. In short, adaptive management seeks to balance conservation with socio-economic stability, allowing fisheries to remain productive and profitable in a warming and more variable environment.
Complementary fish health improvements for both wild populations and aquaculture aim to enhance the biological resilience and disease resistance of fish exposed to thermal stress, hypoxia, acidification, and emerging pathogens. Healthier fish are more robust against climate stressors, leading to lower mortality rates, higher growth and reproduction, and reduced need for emergency closures or antibiotic interventions. At the ecosystem scale, improved fish health supports biodiversity and food-web stability, while in aquaculture it ensures steady yields and economic continuity. Together, effective fisheries regulation and proactive fish-health management deliver a dual adaptation benefit: they reduce vulnerability (by lowering exposure and sensitivity to climate stress) and build adaptive capacity (by enabling recovery and flexibility) across Europe’s fisheries and aquaculture sectors.

2.3.3 Co-benefits for infectious disease
2.3.3.1 Healthy ecosystems
Fishing rules and improved fish health contribute to healthier marine and freshwater ecosystems, which carries important co-benefits for infectious disease risks for both animals and humans. Intact coastal and freshwater ecosystems (seagrass beds, salt marshes, shellfish reefs, and wetlands) perform “pathogen-filtration” and dilution services that lower pathogen concentrations in the water column and sediments. Mechanisms include physical settling of particles (and particle-associated microbes), biofiltration by bivalves, microbial antagonism in vegetated sediments and phyllospheres, and retention/degradation of contaminants in wetland soils. By reducing environmental pathogen loads and smoothing extreme fluctuations (e.g., from runoff pulses), healthy habitats lower exposure opportunities for bathers, shellfish harvests and coastal communities. Fishing rules that protect key habitats (MPAs, gear restrictions, seasonal closures on nursery grounds) and measures that improve wild-stock health (reducing overfishing, restoring age structure) help maintain the ecological functions above. Similarly, restoring wetlands and seagrasses (often supported by fisheries/blue-nature programmes) increases the landscape’s ability to trap and inactivate microbes. Thus, sound fisheries policy is not only about stock biomass but also about preserving ecosystem services that reduce human pathogen exposure.
Examples
	•	Seagrass meadows: Reviews and recent studies show seagrass beds can reduce fecal indicator bacteria (E. coli, enterococci) and suppress potentially pathogenic taxa (including Vibrio spp.) through sedimentation, altered flow and antagonistic microbial communities — implying lower pathogen concentrations near vegetated areas compared with unvegetated seabeds. This has been documented in temperate European waters (Baltic/North Sea studies and GEOMAR work on bacterial communities associated with seagrass). Protecting or restoring seagrass therefore plausibly reduces environmental exposure pathways for human pathogens.
	•	Wetland and shellfish bed services: Syntheses show mangroves/wetlands and bivalve beds remove or transform pathogens and particulate contaminants; policy analyses in Europe advocate wetland restoration as a low-cost complement to engineered wastewater treatment for coastal public health co-benefits. Restoring these features reduces contamination reaching shellfish growing areas and bathing sites.
	•	Relevance to climate change & pathogens such as Vibrio. Climate warming increases the abundance and geographic range of warm-water opportunistic pathogens (e.g., Vibrio spp.), raising risks of wound infections and foodborne illness during heatwaves. Healthy coastal habitats that moderate local temperatures, improve water mixing and reduce pathogen concentrations can partly buffer these climate-driven increases in exposure. The summer 2018–2019 Baltic/Northern Europe heatwaves provide case evidence: unusually warm conditions coincided with increased Vibrio infections in Germany and other Baltic states, and public-health agencies (ECDC) now flag Vibrio as a climate-sensitive hazard in Europe. Protecting/restoring habitats is therefore a tangible co-benefit action.

2.3.3.2 Safe seafood supply
Sustainable fishing rules and healthy fish populations are crucial for ensuring a safe supply of seafood for human consumption. Foodborne infections from seafood arise when pathogens or algal toxins accumulate in edible tissues (bivalves, fish) or contaminate handling/processing chains. Fisheries rules and fish-health measures reduce these risks by: (1) restricting harvesting from contaminated waters (closures), (2) enforcing water-quality standards at production sites, (3) supporting surveillance and pathogen testing, (4) improving aquaculture biosecurity and animal health (lower pathogen prevalence in farms), and (5) strengthening post-harvest handling/depuration/processing rules. Together these actions reduce pathogen loads in the product and the probability of contaminated seafood reaching consumers.
Improving the health of farmed fish (biosecurity, lowered stocking densities, vaccination, better water treatment or use of RAS) reduces within-farm pathogen reservoirs and the chance of spillover to wild stocks or downstream harvest areas. For wild fisheries, reducing overexploitation and avoiding destructive gear lowers incidental transfers of sediment-bound pathogens and preserves natural purification services. Finally, strengthened surveillance (pathogen testing of harvests) and tighter harvest controls ensure contaminated lots are detected and removed before reaching consumers — a direct public-health co-benefit of fish-health programmes.
Examples
European examples / empirical evidence.
	•	Norovirus in oysters (wastewater contamination & closures): Oysters filter and bioaccumulate human enteric viruses such as norovirus. Multiple outbreak investigations across the UK, France, Norway, Sweden and Denmark have linked raw oyster consumption to norovirus clusters; these events are frequently tied to sewage inputs, storm overflows or inadequate wastewater infrastructure in catchments supplying shellfish areas. Regulatory closures, improved wastewater management and targeted monitoring have been central responses. A high-profile French example (Arcachon basin) led to judicially mandated sanitation upgrades after repeated norovirus-linked oyster bans, highlighting how enforcement of environmental and fisheries rules protects public health and markets.
	•	Harmful algal blooms (HABs) and shellfish toxin events: HABs produce toxins (paralytic, amnesic, diarrhetic syndromes) that can accumulate in shellfish or fish and cause severe human illness. European monitoring programmes combined with conditional closures of harvesting areas (and rapid product testing) are standard preventive measures, illustrating how surveillance and spatial closures (fisheries regulation) directly block contaminated products from reaching consumers. Reviews synthesize numerous HAB-linked foodborne illness outbreaks and underline the need for strengthened monitoring as warming expands HAB risk.

2.3.4 Trade-offs for infectious disease
2.3.4.1 Ecological disruption
Well-intentioned fisheries rules and fish-health interventions can, through ecological disruption (habitat damage, altered species interactions, pathogen amplification), create or amplify environmental conditions that raise human infectious-disease risk, particularly for waterborne bacteria (e.g., Vibrio), shellfish-borne viruses/toxins and opportunistic microbial hazards. This trade-off may manifest through different mechanisms:
	•	Effort displacement and concentrated habitat damage. Spatial protections (MPAs, area closures) or seasonal restrictions that are not paired with displacement planning can push fishing effort into new locations where trawling or intensive gear resuspends sediments, destroys benthic community structure, and increases particulate-associated microbial loads. Increased sediment resuspension and habitat degradation change local oxygen and nutrient dynamics, potentially favouring opportunistic bacteria and harmful algal blooms that raise exposure for bathers and shellfish consumers. Modeling and empirical work show closures can reduce harm locally but shift pressure elsewhere unless displacement is anticipated and managed.
	•	Aquaculture amplification and spillover altering pathogen ecologies. Intensification of aquaculture (or failures of containment) can amplify pathogens and parasites in local ecosystems; large, persistent disease reservoirs (e.g., sea-lice, viral agents) may spill back to wild populations and alter host–pathogen dynamics, causing increased mortalities or weakened populations. Such ecosystem perturbations can change decomposition and microbial regimes (more carrion, altered nutrient cycling), which in turn can boost densities of opportunistic bacteria in nearshore waters and sediments that harm human health. Recent reviews and field studies document pathogen amplification linked to salmon aquaculture and disease transfers to wild salmonids.
	•	Trophic cascades and bloom dynamics (jellyfish, thaliaceans, HABs). Removal or suppression of predators/forage fish through fishing or mis-targeted regulations can trigger trophic imbalances that favour gelatinous plankton or other bloom-forming groups. Large gelatinous blooms and subsequent fish kills produce organic loading and hypoxia when biomass decomposes – conditions that change microbial communities and can favour pathogens (or increase toxin-producing algal dominance). Mediterranean and regional analyses link overfishing and ecosystem shifts to more frequent large jellyfish events that have knock-on ecological and socio-economic effects.
	•	Benthic disturbance and loss of cleansing ecosystem functions. Bottom-trawling and other destructive gear remove benthic filterers and degrade seagrass or bivalve beds that normally filter and stabilize microbes and particles. Loss of these ecosystem services reduces natural pathogen attenuation (biofiltration, sediment stabilization) and can increase persistence of fecal indicators or Vibrio in coastal waters, thus increasing exposure risk at bathing sites and shellfish beds. Regional benthic assessments note links between trawling, habitat loss and functional declines.
