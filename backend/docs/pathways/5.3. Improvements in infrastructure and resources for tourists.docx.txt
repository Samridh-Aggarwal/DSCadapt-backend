Improve infrastructures & resources for tourists  Investments in infrastructure and resources for tourists vary greatly in type, scale, and scope. They range from large-scale upgrades to transport networks and increased frequency of travel routes, to the reinforcement of water systems and airport facilities. They may include hiring more staff in key tourist destinations such as national parks, monuments, and museums, as well as smaller-scale interventions like enhanced street signage and additional waste disposal facilities. Improving infrastructure to support tourist flows can also intersect with the health sector, for example by strengthening local hospitals through increased funding, staffing, and the provision of translation services.   In less visited or emerging tourist destinations, such investments may involve building small local health units and accommodation, promoting the development of the hospitality sector, and launching guided tours of national or regional sites. Additional measures could include deploying lifeguards to previously unmonitored areas and financing environmental clean-up operations.   While many of these improvements are directly targeted at tourists, their impact – be it viewed as beneficial or disruptive – often extends to local communities. For instance, the construction of a new train line is likely to affect residents as much as visitors.  5.3.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change  While investments in infrastructure and resources for tourists are largely developed to attract travellers or to respond to rising visitor numbers, they may equally serve as adaptation strategies to climate change.   Climate change is expected to have a significant impact on the tourism sector, as rising temperatures, sea-level rise, and the increasing frequency of droughts and floods threaten the viability of traditional travel patterns and tourism seasons. Tourism, while sometimes viewed by locals as a burden, is a vital economic force for many European countries, and the effects of climate change could lead to the restructuring of travel patterns and diminished viability of certain destinations. For example, declining snow cover in mountainous regions could jeopardise winter tourism, while sea level rise places coastal tourism at risk. Extreme heat throughout Europe can equally provoke significant losses for warmer tourist destinations.   To help maintain visitor numbers and stimulate continued tourism in threatened regions, infrastructure and resource improvements will form an essential part of adaptation strategies. Such improvements may support the expansion of tourism into new locations, into more temperature or climate-resilient areas that offer consistently comfortable conditions for travellers. These efforts can help to sustain tourism levels, offsetting the economic losses associated with traditional destinations becoming less viable or attractive due to heat, drought, and other climate-induced disruptions.  
Alongside development in new and less-visited regions, enhancing infrastructure in vulnerable existing destinations is equally critical for improving the overall resilience of the tourism sector. These measures will aim to increase the comfort and safety of tourists, particularly during periods of extreme weather. For example, cities experiencing severe heat may install and signpost air-conditioned ‘cooling stations’ near major attractions to prevent health issues among visitors.   More broadly, infrastructure improvements that comprehensively bolster local resilience, such as upgrades to roads, water supply systems, energy networks, and transport services, are key to safeguarding tourism from climate impacts. In some cases, these upgrades may specifically target components of these systems that directly serve the tourism sector. Policies may focus on securing water and energy resources in specific tourist hotspots, for example, to help ensure that traditional destinations remain operational and attractive.   Beyond physical infrastructure, climate-specific improvements for tourists may also include informational resources: guidance on optimal travel times, health risk management (e.g. heat exposure or mosquito-borne illnesses), and water safety. These awareness-raising measures that educate tourists on how to navigate changing climate conditions can complement physical investments in supporting tourism-related climate adaptation. 
 By investing in targeted infrastructure and resources improvements, regions can strengthen the resilience of the tourism sector to the impacts of climate change and reduce the risks of economic losses tied to the diminishing viability and appeal of traditional destinations.   5.3.2. 5.3.3. Co-benefit of reduced pressure on health services   Increasing tourist numbers without corresponding investment in healthcare infrastructure can compromise a region’s resilience to infectious diseases. By contrast, directing infrastructure and resource improvements for tourism toward the health sector can ease pressure on local services, strengthening both public health capacity and its resilience to infectious disease.  Healthcare investments aimed at mitigating the strain of increased visitor numbers may take multiple forms, including boosting hospital funding, increasing staff, and introducing translation services to improve accessibility. In less visited or emerging destinations, investments may involve establishing small local health units, which can benefit both tourists and residents by improving the general accessibility of timely care.   Beyond physical infrastructure, climate-specific health resources for tourists may also include informational resources: guidance on optimal travel times, health risk management (e.g. heat exposure or mosquito-borne illnesses), and water safety. These awareness-raising measures can educate tourists on how to navigate climate conditions, helping to prevent avoidable illness and reducing demand on local health systems, thus preserving their ability to serve the resident population effectively.   Simply, robust, well-funded and prepared healthcare systems are critical for maintaining disease resilience; strengthening the capacity of local health infrastructure to handle increasing numbers of tourists helps protect both visitors and host communities, by maintaining its ability to respond to infectious disease risks.   As climate change patterns are expected to increase the pressures on public health systems, (through incidences such as extreme weather events, water scarcity, and ecosystem disruption), ensuring that healthcare infrastructure is not further burdened by new or increasing tourism becomes increasingly important. Investing in improving healthcare in tourist destinations therefore offers essential benefits for infectious disease risk and the overall resilience of local health services.
  5.3.4. Co-benefit of contributing to healthy ecosystems  If investments in infrastructure and resources for tourists are directed towards developing attractive natural areas, they may support the conservation, clean-up, restoration, or rewilding of specific environments. Such investments align with broader trends in ‘sustainable’, nature-based, or ‘regenerative’ tourism, which promote environmental stewardship alongside economic development. These approaches, particularly nature-based solutions (NBS), are recognised for their potential to preserve, and even restore the functionality of ecosystems, while delivering tangible social and economic benefits. NBS, in turn, are being progressively integrated into policy frameworks and adaptation planning, including within the EU’s Natura 2000 network, established under the Nature Restoration Law.   Where these tourism-oriented investments contribute to the recovery and overall health of local ecosystems, they may also yield important co-benefits for resilience to infectious diseases. Biodiversity and ecological balance play a critical role in maintaining ecosystem resilience: in degraded ecosystems and areas of weakened genetic and species diversity, the complexity and heterogeneity of the natural system are compromised, which makes it more vulnerable and at a heightened risk of creating new opportunities for disease emergence. Notably, the risk of infectious zoonotic diseases is often linked to the degradation or destruction of ecosystems. Consequently, investing in the preservation and restoration of natural areas, in ways that maintain and strengthen ecosystem health, can help limit the presence and spread of disease vectors, thereby reducing the risk of infectious disease.    This benefit is especially pronounced when investments target previously neglected or degraded areas, where interventions are more likely to significantly improve overall ecosystem health and their capacity to mitigate disease threats.   Moreover, if tourism-focused improvements help attract ongoing financial support to areas – financial support that relies on their being preserved as healthy, natural spaces – they may also shield these ecosystems from alternative, environmentally destructive economic uses, such as mining, intensive agriculture, or industrial development. For example, maintaining biodiversity to sustain birdwatching activities in a thriving park is likely to secure a healthier, and thus more resilient ecosystem, than extractive alternatives. In this way, investments in tourist infrastructure that are explicitly oriented toward the preservation or enhancement of natural areas can further serve to strengthen infectious disease resilience.   5.3.5. Trade-off of ecological disruption 
 Improvements in infrastructure and resources for tourists can involve development, construction, and resource use that disrupt surrounding ecosystems. This is especially true when such infrastructure is built in or near previously undisturbed natural environments or ecologically rich areas, where human activity has historically been minimal. Improvements can also make specific destinations more accessible and attractive to tourists, increasing visitor numbers and amplifying the strain on local ecosystems. These disruptions can diminish ecosystem resilience, carrying significant trade-offs for infectious disease resilience.   The adverse impacts of tourists on local environments stem not only from their physical presence, but equally from the ecological pressures their presence introduces: direct environmental impacts include trampling, wildlife disturbance, and littering, while broader indirect or cumulative effects include increased air and water pollution, reduced freshwater availability, greater wastewater output, loss of forest cover, and accelerated soil erosion. Even forms of ‘ecotourism’ are considered to carry risks of ecosystem depletion, particularly when they involve the consumption of virgin territories, as they are much more likely to involve deforestation and significant disruption of ecological life systems. Inexperienced, or non-local tourists, unfamiliar with the sensitivities of particular ecosystems, can also heighten the risk of catastrophic sudden events, such as human-induced wildfires.   When tourist-related improvements exert pressure on natural resources, promote development in previously undisturbed areas, and increase pollution, waste, and human-wildlife interactions, they place ecosystems at risk of disruption and degradation - an ecological disruption which has direct implications for disease resilience. Biodiverse and healthy ecosystems provide a range of habitats and promote genetic variability, creating natural checks on the spread of pathogens; variability in natural systems buffers against disease transmission by preventing the dominance of single disease vectors or hosts. When biodiversity is lost, this resilience weakens. In fact, ecosystem degradation and biodiversity loss have been identified as the primary environmental driver of infectious diseases.   Consequently, efforts to improve tourist resources and services through infrastructure projects or even nature-based developments, particularly when expanding into previously less-visited areas, risk disrupting and degrading ecosystem health. Such disturbances can compromise biodiversity and ecological integrity, inadvertently heightening vulnerability to infectious disease outbreaks.
 5.3.6. Trade-off of social unrest, displacement of local communities, mental health risks   Investments in infrastructure, services, and resources for tourists can inadvertently undermine community stability, potentially increasing vulnerability to infectious disease.  Infrastructure developments may risk displacing local communities, weakening small businesses, and disrupting traditional socioeconomic structures, livelihoods, and cultural knowledge systems - whether through the construction of large-scale tourist facilities, the expansion of transport networks that cut through residential or natural areas, or the installation of energy-intensive amenities like water parks, golf courses, and luxury resorts, these transformations can threaten community stability. The redirection of local funding toward tourism infrastructure may also divert resources away from essential public services, for example, by prioritising improvements to transport and health services along tourist routes rather than in long-established residential areas.  Over time, increasing tourism, gentrification, and displacement is likely to provoke social unrest and mental health concerns among affected populations, both of which carry consequences for infectious disease risk. Across Europe, rising tourist numbers are linked to growing public resistance, marked by anti-tourism protests and sentiments of tourism-phobia. Displacement and unrest can increase exposure to infectious diseases, whether it manifests through relocation to concentrated housing developments, increased local population mobility, or public demonstrations. These conditions can increase the risk of infectious disease transmission, as population density facilitates the spread of gastrointestinal and respiratory infections.   Moreover, displacement and social unrest can trigger or exacerbate stress, anxiety, depression and other mental health conditions. These impacts are not only harmful in themselves but also reduce human resilience to disease, as poor mental health can impair immune function, reducing the body’s ability to combat pathogens and potentially leading to more severe disease outcomes. In this way, investments in infrastructure and resources for tourists, when linked with displacement and social unrest, may inadvertently heighten human vulnerability to infectious diseases due to the mental health burden these can impose.
