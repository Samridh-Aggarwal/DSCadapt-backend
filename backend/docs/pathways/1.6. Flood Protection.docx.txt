Flood protection  Flood protection is a key component of flood risk management and the response to flooding events.  
The 2007 European Floods Directive requires all Member States to develop flood risk management plans that address every aspect of flood risk, including prevention, protection, and preparedness, including flood forecasting and early warning systems (FFEWS). EU countries must identify areas at risk of significant flooding, map the potential flood extent and the people and assets at risk, and implement adequate, coordinated measures to reduce flood risk. According to the European Commission’s Flood Map Viewer, which compiles data reported by Member States under the Directive, more than 14,000 areas in the EU are currently considered at significant risk of flooding.
 A wide range of measures fall under the umbrella of flood protection. As outlined in the EU Climate Risk Assessment, these include flood defence measures (grey), that modify physical infrastructure; nature-based solutions (green); and flood preparedness strategies and early warning systems, often classified as soft measures. These collectively aim to reduce the severity and impact of flooding, as well as the risk of exposure to floodwaters.   Additionally, zoning restrictions, development regulations, and retreat strategies related to floodplains, (including relocation or resettlement), can be considered indirect flood protection measures. Among these, retreat measures are particularly effective for flood protection and risk avoidance, but they are often culturally and socially sensitive, and frequently face resistance from communities.   Essentially, flood protection measures fall into two main categories: hazard-reducing measures, which aim to lessen the frequency and magnitude of flood events, and flood risk reduction practices, which focus on decreasing exposure or vulnerability to flooding, such as through relocation or dry-proofing, rather than addressing the flood hazard itself.     Economic losses from flooding can reach into the billions. Although flood protection measures may seem costly, studies show that their financial benefits can outweigh post-disaster rebuilding costs by up to ten times. European countries are widely regarded as having some of the highest flood protection standards in the world, and by 2100, it is estimated that effective flood management could reduce annual projected flood losses in Europe by up to €36 billion and decrease the exposed population by 84%. 

1.6.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change  Flood protection measures have contributed to reducing the extent of flooding and the severity of flood-related impacts across Europe for centuries. A notable example is the MOSE system in Venice, Italy, an integrated network of mobile gates installed on the seafloor, designed to temporarily seal off the Venetian Lagoon from the Adriatic Sea during acqua alta (high tide) events.  However, flooding still occurs, and with climate change, the risk of floods is increasing. Climate data indicate that heavy precipitation is becoming more frequent in Europe, particularly in northern regions and the Alpine areas. There are also increasing trends of river flooding in western and central Europe, while accelerated sea-level rise in coastal areas is heightening the risk of coastal floods and storm surges. Northern and western-central European cities are expected to a experience a significant increase in both pluvial and fluvial floods, whereas southern European cities, more prone to increasing drought, are becoming increasingly vulnerable to sudden, intense precipitation events occurring during dry periods.  Consequently, flood protection measures are considered a critical form of adaptation and response to the growth in flood risks associated with climate change.

  1.6.3. Co-benefit of reducing exposure to floodwater  Whether grey, green, soft, or zoning-related, flood protection measures aim to lessen the severity and consequences of flooding, and in turn, the risk of exposure to floodwater. In doing so, they offer crucial co-benefits for infectious disease transmission risk.   Floodwaters are often contaminated, containing pathogens from sources such as surface run-off, sewer overflows, and even animal waste. These pathogens raise the risk of outbreaks of waterborne diseases through direct or indirect contact with contaminated water following flood events. Accordingly, flood protection measures that help mitigate pluvial and fluvial flooding, prevent sewer overflows, and limit the release and spread of floodwater equally serve to reduce exposure to infectious pathogens, and thus limit the risk of waterborne illnesses.   Targeted flood protection measures that safeguard water infrastructure can also help ensure continued access to clean water for essential hygiene practices and public health, both of which are critical for preventing the transmission of waterborne illnesses.   Beyond waterborne diseases, floodwaters can also elevate the risk of vector-borne diseases; vast areas of standing or stagnant water following flooding can create ideal breeding conditions for mosquitoes, thus increasing the risk of mosquito-borne diseases such as malaria, dengue fever, and West Nile virus. By reducing exposure to floodwater, protection measures can also limit the formation of these habitats, and thus reduce the risk of mosquito-borne infections.
 Finally, public communications on flood protection and flood risk awareness can further support infectious disease prevention; these inform the public about individual protective actions, and often include health-related messaging, raising awareness of post-flood disease risks and promoting early preventive behaviours. Flood protection communications can therefore strengthen community resilience to infectious diseases and contribute to both immediate and long-term health protection.  
1.6.4. Co-benefit of contributing to healthy ecosystems  Certain flood protection measures that employ nature-based solutions (NBS) or green approaches, such as wetland restoration or the reconnection of floodplains, can offer the added co-benefit of supporting infectious disease resilience by contributing to the health and stability of ecosystems.   NBS are recognised as a sustainable means of preserving or restoring the functionality of natural systems; by enhancing nature and ecosystem processes, they can deliver environmental, social, and economic benefits. NBS are recognised for their potential to address both the climate and biodiversity crises, and are increasingly integrated into policy frameworks and conservation and adaptation planning, including within the EU’s Natura 2000 network, established under the Nature Restoration Law.   NBS solutions have the potential to improve and restore biodiversity and ecological balance, both of which are associated with greater resilience to infectious disease. In degraded ecosystems and areas of weakened genetic and species diversity, the complexity and heterogeneity of the natural system are compromised, which makes it more vulnerable and at a heightened risk of creating new opportunities for disease emergence. Notably, the risk of infectious zoonotic diseases is often linked to the degradation or destruction of ecosystems. Consequently, preserving and strengthening ecosystem health can help limit the presence and spread of disease vectors, thereby reducing the risk of infectious disease.  
 Nature-based or green flood protection measures often specifically target the recovery and restoration of natural watercourse morphology and dynamics, as well as the protection of water quality, aquifer recharge, and the improvement of ecological connectivity. Indeed, restorative flood management approaches, such as the restoration of functional floodplains and riverine habitats, can have major positive effects on biodiversity and the provision of ecosystem services. In this way, nature-based flood protection can contribute to healthier ecosystems that, in turn, enhance resilience to infectious diseases.  More broadly, beyond NBS and restoring ecosystem health, certain flood protection measures can shield natural ecosystems from the damaging effects of flooding itself. Flood events can cause extreme damage to natural ecosystems and hamper their ability to deliver ecosystem services; by reducing the impact of flooding on sensitive natural areas, flood protection can help preserve ecosystem integrity and functions, reinforcing ecosystem health and, in turn, reducing the risk of infectious diseases.   1.6.5. Co-benefit of safe seafood supply  Whether grey, green, soft, or zoning-related, flood protection measures aim to lessen the severity and consequences of flooding, and in turn, the risk of floodwaters entering nearby waters (both fresh and coastal) and negatively impacting marine life and local aquaculture. In doing so, they can help to secure the safety of the seafood supply, preventing pathogens present in contaminated floodwaters from affecting local fish and mollusc populations, thus reducing the risk of seafood-associated infectious disease outbreaks.  Floodwaters are often contaminated, containing pathogens from sources such as surface runoff, sewer overflows, and agricultural and animal waste. These pathogens raise the risk of outbreaks of diseases of concern for human health in seafood, such as Vibrio bacteria. Accordingly, flood protection measures that help mitigate pluvial and fluvial flooding, prevent overflows and runoff, and limit the release and spread of floodwater equally serve to reduce seafood’s exposure to pathogens and thus limit the risk of infectious disease outbreaks.  In general, contact of local fish populations, whether wild or within aquaculture, with floodwaters is likely to deteriorate water quality. Pathogen loads can rise greatly in aquaculture systems when poor water quality prevails, critically increasing susceptibility to infections in sea creatures. This creates a land-to-sea transfer of infectious agents, particularly bacterial microorganisms such as Salmonella spp., Escherichia coli, Vibrio cholerae, and Listeria monocytogenes. Once transferred into marine waters via flood-derived mud and debris, microbial pathogens may be ingested by marine species, which is a particular concern for edible bivalve molluscs like mussels - these creatures feed by filtering large amounts of water, and thus bioconcentrate any pollutants present in their environment, which has led to viral concentrations in shellfish 100 times higher than in the surrounding seawater, as well as significant amounts of biological and chemical environmental pollutants.  Consequently, flooding overflows and untreated sewage flowing into coastal waters can be associated with outbreaks of seafood-associated infectious disease, as was the case for several outbreaks of norovirus-linked gastroenteritis in France traced back to oyster harvesting. Flooding has also been associated with a decrease in the salinity of coastal waters, creating a habitat in which Vibrio bacteria are more likely to thrive and multiply, creating an environment that elevates the risk of infection for sea creatures. Furthermore, preliminary studies show that flood severity and frequency have been associated with lower profitability among fish farmers, leading to recommendations for adaptation measures to reduce flooding risks, such as siting aquaculture further away from flood-prone areas.  Consequently, flood protection measures that prevent local fish and mollusc populations from coming into contact with floodwaters can reduce the potential for infectious disease emergence in the seafood supply down the line.

 1.6.6. Co-benefit of improved food safety   Extreme weather events can directly compromise food safety: flooding, heavy rainfall and hurricanes can facilitate the spread of bacterial and viral contaminants throughout the food chain, as floodwaters containing sewage, animal waste and agricultural runoff provide ideal conditions for the proliferation of waterborne pathogens, including norovirus, E. coli, Giardiasis, Hepatitis A and Campylobacteriosis. Consequently, flood protection measures that lessen the severity and consequences of flooding, and in turn, the risk of floodwaters contaminating local agriculture, can help reduce the risk of water- and foodborne infectious disease outbreaks.  
 
When floodwaters overflow, runoff from livestock, industrial, and residential areas can contaminate agricultural water, fields, and processing facilities. This can lead to an increase in faecal indicators, such as E. Coli, and the detection of environmental pathogens such as Listeria and Salmonella in agricultural produce. Flooding has been frequently linked to outbreaks of foodborne and waterborne gastrointestinal illness from fresh horticultural produce; these crops are grown in open fields and exposed to the elements, rendering them more vulnerable to microbial contamination and related food safety risks. An example of this is the 2022 outbreak of Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli in the UK, which was associated with lettuce contaminated by sheep faeces washed to the field by heavy rainfall and flooding.
 
Consequently, targeted flood protection measures that safeguard agricultural crops from contaminated floodwaters can help prevent the emergence of water- and foodborne infectious diseases. Protecting water infrastructure from flood risks is of particular importance, as continued access to clean water is essential for maintaining critical food safety practices like washing produce and hands, which reduces the risk of water- and foodborne illnesses.


1.6.5. Trade-off of ecological disruption  By altering natural ecosystems and manipulating water flows, certain flood protection measures carry the trade-off of potentially increasing infectious disease risk through ecological disruption.   Traditional grey protection measures, such as dykes, levees, and floodwalls, aim to control water flow and prevent flooding. However, this control often interferes with natural hydrological processes, reducing river connectivity and altering water fluctuations. These water flows affect aquatic systems, shaping habitats, distributing nutrients, and supporting the life cycles of numerous species. As a result, such interventions can lead to habitat degradation and biodiversity loss, undermining ecosystem integrity.   This ecological disruption has direct implications for disease resilience. Biodiverse and healthy ecosystems provide a range of habitats and promote genetic variability, which can create natural checks on the spread of pathogens; variability in natural systems buffers against disease transmission by preventing the dominance of single disease vectors or hosts. When biodiversity is lost, this resilience weakens. In fact, ecosystem degradation and biodiversity loss have been identified as the primary environmental driver of infectious diseases.  Consequently, flood protection measures that compromise ecosystem health may inadvertently increase vulnerability to disease outbreaks.   A case in point is the MOSE flood barrier in Venice. While effective in shielding the city from high tides, it prevents the regular flooding of salt marshes, reducing sediment deposition necessary for sustaining marsh habitats. This, in turn, risks undermining biodiversity in these areas.   Even green flood protection strategies, such as nature-based solutions (NBS), can pose potential trade-offs. While NBS aim to restore or enhance particular ecosystem services, they may unintentionally disrupt others. By selectively modifying ecosystem functions to address specific concerns, they can alter natural processes, habitats and resource availability, potentially creating changes in species composition and abundance. If short-sightedly designed, such interventions risk reducing biodiversity and thereby potentially weakening ecosystem resilience to infectious disease outbreaks.   1.6.6. Trade-off of creating habitat for disease vectors   Various types of flood protection measures carry the trade-off of potentially creating habitats for disease vectors. Some flood protection measures can cause areas of standing or stagnant water, for example, standing water may accumulate behind dams (grey measure) or proliferate in restored wetlands (nature-based, green measure). Vast areas of stagnant water can create ideal breeding conditions for mosquitoes, thus increasing the risk of mosquito-borne diseases such as malaria, dengue fever, and West Nile virus.    By inadvertently creating habitats that support the proliferation of vectors of infectious diseases, certain flood protection measures can therefore increase the risk of disease outbreaks.   1.6.7. Trade-off of social unrest, displacement and mental health risks

Flood protection through zoning measures, while effective in reducing exposure to flooding, may carry trade-offs for infectious disease risk, particularly due to displacement and its impact on mental health and human vulnerability to disease. 
Flood protection strategies such as retreat, relocation, and resettlement are culturally and socially complex, and often poorly understood or accepted by affected communities. These forms of displacement, especially when implemented with little warning, can increase the likelihood of negative mental health effects. Evacuees, in particular, are prone to experiencing a severe degree of stress. This poses a concern for infectious disease risk, as displacement-related stress can impair immune function, reducing the body’s ability to combat pathogens and potentially leading to more severe disease outcomes. In this way, flood protection measures involving displacement may undermine human resilience to infectious diseases through the mental health burden they impose.   Additionally, evacuation, retreat, and potentially relocation measures may result in crowding in temporary shelters, transitional housing, or newly developed settlements. Such conditions can increase the risk of infectious disease transmission, as higher population density facilitates the spread of gastrointestinal and respiratory infections.   Consequently, displacement-based flood protection measures, whether temporary, long-term, or permanent, can heighten human vulnerability to disease, both by increasing mental health risks that may weaken immune responses, and by raising the likelihood of disease transmission through close contact, thereby reducing overall resilience to infectious diseases.
