3.6.3. (Co-)benefit of improved food safety   3.6. Climate resilient food safety systems and regulations   Food safety systems and regulations encompass the entire food chain, governing activities from production and processing through storage, transportation, sale and eventual consumption. These frameworks apply to all categories of food – fresh, refrigerated and shelf-stable – and set out specific standards, monitoring procedures and enforcement mechanisms to ensure appropriate handling, storage and preparation. They are designed to safeguard both public health and the integrity of food supply systems, establishing hygiene and safety requirements across all sectors of the food economy.   Globally, food safety systems are informed by international standards, such as those developed by the Codex Alimentarius Commission, a joint initiative of the Food and Agricultural Organisation (FAO) and the World Health Organisation (WHO). These standards provide a scientific foundation for food legislation, ensuring that food is safe, of acceptable quality, and can be traded fairly across borders. National and regional food safety authorities integrate these principles into regulatory frameworks.   3.6.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change 
Climate change is expected to profoundly disrupt food systems, influencing not only the production of food but also its distribution, quality and safety. Changes in soil fertility, increasing temperatures and the frequency of extreme weather events such as floods and droughts threaten agricultural productivity, reducing yields and constraining the affordability and availability of food. Critically, extreme weather events can directly compromise food safety: flooding, heavy rainfall and hurricanes can facilitate the spread of bacterial and viral contaminants throughout the food chain, as floodwaters containing sewage, animal waste and agricultural run-off provide ideal conditions for the proliferation of waterborne pathogens, including norovirus, E. coli, Giardiasis, Hepatitis A and Campylobacteriosis.   Rising ambient temperatures and increased humidity further jeopardise food safety by stimulating greater microbial activity within food systems. Foodborne pathogens such as Salmonella, E. coli, and listeria demonstrate accelerated growth and longer survival in warmer environments, increasing the speed and scale at which food contamination can occur. Collectively, climate-induced changes influence the abundance, growth, geographic range and survival of foodborne pathogens, elevating risks across all stages of the food chain, from processing and transport to storage and consumption, and amplifying the likelihood of outbreaks of foodborne illness. 
Additionally, extreme heat and severe weather can undermine the stability of infrastructure critical to maintaining food safety. Power outages or equipment failures during heatwaves, storms or flooding can disrupt and disable refrigeration systems and cold storage facilities, leading to food spoilage and heightening the risk of contamination. Coastal flooding and storm surges can inflict further damage on storage facilities and distribution networks. For instance, following Hurricane Sandy in 2012, disruptions to refrigeration systems caused an outbreak of salmonellosis in the north-eastern United States. Higher temperatures, increased humidity and rising sea levels also place stress on food system infrastructure, including machinery, transport and telecommunications, which collectively create vulnerabilities throughout the food chain. An example includes the deterioration of livestock feed quality under warmer and more humid conditions, resulting in increased post-harvest losses at the farm level and potentially driving the growth of harmful moulds and bacteria.   Broadly, climate change-driven decreases in biodiversity and alterations to ecosystems further increase the risk of zoonotic disease emergence: habitat disruption and shifts in species distribution can increase contact between wildlife and livestock, parasite transmission and heightening the possibility of zoonotic spillover. As witnessed during the COVID-19 pandemic, zoonotic pathogens can persist in domesticated animals and cross species barriers, with severe implications for global health.    Given these interconnected risks, it is of critical importance that food systems adapt. Strengthening and modernising national and regional food safety frameworks to incorporate climate resilience objectives can help reduce the risk of contamination and control the spread of foodborne pathogens. Measures including robust prevention strategies, enhanced food safety monitoring, early warning systems, and coordinated rapid response mechanisms are all vital to respond to foodborne disease risks and to mitigate the public health impacts of climate-related disruptions to the food system. 


3.6.2. Adaptation measures  The improvement and updating of food safety systems and regulations to render them as climate-resilient as possible is a food-chain-wide endeavour that encompasses a broad array of measures. Strict regulation, coupled with rigorous enforcement of standards and rules, is essential to ensure implementation and to underpin improvements in food safety systems. At the regional level, the European Animal Health Law  and the Plant Health Law  both highlight the need to assess and address climate-driven risks to crops, forests, and farm animals. This legislation forms part of a wider network of European food safety regulations, supported by national legislation, guidelines and voluntary measures. At every stage of food production, from processing to transport, storage, sale and preparation, food safety encompasses prevention, monitoring, containment, and response.   Prevention requires a comprehensive assessment of foodborne disease risks under changing climate conditions. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA)’s Climate change and Emerging Risks for Food Safety (CLEFSA) project, for instance, investigates how scenario analysis, crowdsourced and text-mined data, expert networks and decision-analysis tools can help anticipate emerging food safety risks driven by climate change in Europe. CLEFSA has identified a variety of issues and, importantly, recommends that policymakers and food system actors adjust surveillance and monitoring in preparation for new and augmented risks caused by climate change. The project also emphasises the need for closer collaboration among policymakers, risk assessors, researchers, and the food system more broadly, as well as better sharing of scientific data.   Indeed, improved information exchange is central to bolstering food safety systems as an adaptation strategy; early warning systems are especially critical in containing the spread of foodborne pathogens under new climatic pressures. In Europe, the Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (RASFF) ensures rapid information exchange between  Member Stages when health risks are identified – whether by national authorities in agri-food inspections, or by companies undertaking their own analyses – and facilitates prompt responses when food-chain risks to public health emerge. Notifications via RASFF’s platform facilitates the swift recall of unsafe products from the market, helping to support rapid containment of foodborne pathogens.   Prevention also includes addressing and improving the environmental conditions in which food is produced and distributed to mitigate heightened risks driven by climate change. Possible measures include using solar power to maintain reliable electricity for cold-chain storage, cleaning storage spaces more frequently to counter rapid bacterial growth and proliferation under heat and humidity, increasing the frequency of temperature checks for stored food, and verifying the accuracy of refrigerator and freezer gauges with thermometers.  Many similar preventive actions, particularly in restaurants and other food service settings, are already embedded in national and regional food safety legislation, which outlines standards for food handling and mandates monitoring, analysis, and the prompt reporting of identified risks. Among the most widely recognised and applied frameworks is the Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) system, a preventative framework for food safety that has been proven effective even in the face of climate change impacts. For instance, applying HACCP-Quantitative Microbiological Risk Assessment has been proposed as a potential instrument to manage the effects of climate change on food quality and safety.  With regard to monitoring measures, improvements might include regular and weather-dependent testing for contamination (for instance, increased sampling during periods of high temperature and humidity). Monitoring systems can also be upgraded: a recent report by the Scientific Committee of the Spanish Agency for Food Safety and Nutrition (AESAN) on the impact of climate change on foodborne pathogen transmission refers to real-time monitoring systems and biosensors as effective improvements to food safety practices. The report also refers to microbiological control technologies as pivotal – these are techniques aimed at detecting, eliminating or inhibiting the proliferation of pathogenic organisms if food and water, or on contact surfaces, such as advanced filtration, UV disinfection, ozone treatment and chlorination. Such technologies enable rapid detection and response to microbiological contaminants in food and water.   Improving response could include strengthening the preparedness of healthcare systems to recognise, report, and treat foodborne diseases whose incidence may rise during certain weather conditions or seasons. This could be supported by enhanced laboratory capacity in public hospitals, effective disease surveillance and communication with health sector workers, and stronger coordination between public health and food safety authorities to ensure the rapid detection and containment of outbreaks.  Finally, a central tenet of strengthening food safety systems and regulations is enhancing surveillance and research capacities to support the continuous and detailed assessment of foodborne disease risks. The very research underpinning this text exemplifies the type of scientific analysis needed to identify shifting distributions, rising incidences, and the emergence of foodborne pathogens in different geographic areas. Such work allows European public health frameworks, food system actors, and national authorities to more effectively prioritise and allocate resources towards context-specific foodborne risks. Examples include strengthening food safety oversight and expanding molecular surveillance of food originating from regions experiencing rapid environmental change. Research also emphasises the value of investing in developing new tools and technologies to detect, trace, and manage foodborne pathogens in a changing environment.    Ultimately, a wide range of measures exist to strengthen food safety systems and regulations in response to climate change. Updating and making these systems climate-resilient enhances overall food safety by reducing the risks of emergence and proliferation of foodborne pathogens, protecting the food chain from climate-related hazards, and preventing public health crises arising from the food system.

3.6.3. (Co-)benefit of improved food safety  
Strengthening and modernising national and regional food safety systems and regulations to incorporate climate resilience objectives can help reduce contamination risks and limit the spread of foodborne pathogens. Updating food safety frameworks to account for the heightened threat of foodborne diseases associated with specific weather patterns, seasonal variations, and climate change more broadly – together with a robust enforcement of regulations – can reduce the emergence and proliferation of pathogens responsible for these illnesses. In doing so, these measures contribute to improved food safety and the protection of public health.  Collectively, food safety systems and regulations target a broad spectrum of foodborne pathogens. As such, their improvement offers wide-ranging benefits, effectively reducing the risk of numerous infectious diseases sensitive to climate change, whether exacerbated by higher temperatures, increased humidity, weather-driven contamination or general damage to food system infrastructure. These include bacterial, viral, and parasitic infections such as Campylobacteriosis, Cryptosporidiosis, E. coli, Giardiasis, Hepatitis A, Listeriosis, Norovirus, Salmonellosis, Shigellosis, and Vibriosis. 
As these diseases are influenced by changing climatic conditions, strengthening and updating food safety systems and regulations as part of climate adaptation strategies builds sector-wide resilience while also directly improving food safety and thus reducing the overall risks of infectious foodborne illnesses.
