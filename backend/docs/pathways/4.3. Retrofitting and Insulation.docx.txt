 4.3. Retrofitting and insulation of buildings  4.3.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change 
Climate change is increasing the incidence in Europe of health hazards, created by extreme weather events, prolonged periods of high temperatures and drought, and even the expanded presence of climate-sensitive disease vectors such as mosquitoes; it is therefore critical that buildings be best equipped to protect occupants from these escalating risks.
 As existing buildings comprise the majority of the building stock, improving their resilience must be prioritised alongside climate-adapted design in new construction.  Retrofitting offers an effective route to improving the energy efficiency and thermal comfort of existing buildings. Building retrofitting interventions can include a wide range of improvements to the building envelope (walls, roofs, doors, windows, ceilings and foundation), by incorporating insulation, efficient building windows, upgraded HVAC systems, energy-efficiency appliances and lighting systems, and improving overall air tightness.   Retrofitting strategies may be grouped into categories that target specific, though frequently overlapping, objectives. Green retrofitting techniques, for example, focus holistically on improved energy and environmental performance, reduced water use, improved comfort and quality of space regarding light, air quality, and noise. Smart retrofitting techniques incorporate intelligent control systems and automation of interconnected components to make efficient use of hardware, remote controls and sensor networks, forecasting future states of a building and making operational decisions without the need for human intervention.   Recent research also stresses the importance of passive retrofitting measures in response to future climate conditions, especially given the project rise in global temperatures. Techniques such as solar shading, and natural ventilation during day and night, should be considered in adaptive retrofitting strategies as they can be highly effective at mitigating overheating and improving indoor thermal comfort. In fact, in colder regions, the integration of proper ventilation techniques and passive cooling technologies may even eliminate overheating risks entirely.  

(Many of these measures are detailed and explored further in 4.2. Building Standards, Regulations and Guidelines). 
 By improving the building’s capacity to maintain stable indoor temperatures, retrofitting reduces reliance on mechanical heating and cooling, with added benefits for emissions reduction and cost savings – in this way, retrofitting embodies a dual-purpose measure, supporting both climate adaptation and mitigation. Measures that minimise air leakage and retain conditioned air directly contribute to a building’s overall climate resilience and sustainability. Indeed, studies analysing the performance of retrofitting under future climate scenarios have found that combining passive and active upgrades can reduce total building energy consumption by up to 38%, depending on the specific climate and building type.

Importantly, building’s energy performance and resilience will evolve under changing climate conditions. For this reason, retrofitting strategies should be informed by future climate modelling, to design accurate performance benchmarks and ensure that existing buildings are well-prepared for multiple decades to come.

4.3.3. Co-benefit of improved indoor & outdoor air quality 
When retrofitting measures are designed with both energy-efficiency and health parameters in mind, they can offer the added benefit of reducing infectious disease risk by improving indoor and outdoor air quality. Certain retrofit strategies specifically target the installation of energy-efficient HVAC technologies, often as part of broader modernisation efforts within ‘smart’ or ‘green’ retrofit programmes; these approaches can integrate targeted objectives for improving air quality, contributing to healthier built environments. 
 Indoor Air Quality   Ventilation of buildings is essential to ensure indoor–outdoor air exchange, preventing the stagnation and build-up of indoor pollutants and contaminants. Air conditioning systems that only regulate temperature, without incorporating ventilation, can even worsen indoor pollution levels, making proper air exchange essential for maintaining indoor air quality. However, air exchange often carries significant energy costs, as incoming outdoor air must typically be heated, cooled or dehumidified, and increased air flow can weaken the effects of internal climate control. Consequently, under current energy performance standards, energy-efficient retrofits may rely on mechanical rather than conventional, ‘natural’ ventilation, i.e., opening doors and windows.   These systems can offer added benefits for indoor air quality: uncontrolled or ‘conventional’ window opening is unreliable and may fail to maintain hygiene air exchange rates, resulting in elevated CO2 concentrations, whereas mechanical ventilation can filter incoming air, and modulate air flow relative to the occupancy, temperature, humidity and pollutant levels. Mechanical ventilation can also be combined with measures such as optimised demand-controlled ventilation and heat recovery systems, capable of conserving energy while improving indoor air quality.  Indeed, mechanical ventilation in energy-efficient buildings has been shown to lower concentrations of chemical pollutants and biological contaminants compared to conventional ventilation, including volatile organic compounds, CO2, radon and mould spores. This is, in part, attributed to higher air-exchange rates which remove pollutants more effectively, and better moisture control which keeps relative humidity below thresholds that could favour harmful fungal growth. Modern HVAC (Heating, Ventilation and Air Conditioning) systems can even be programmed to adjust ventilation rates based on detected pollutant concentration (and other control parameters), a requirement for certain units specified in the EU’s Directive on ecodesign requirements for ventilation units.   Regular inspection and maintenance of heating, ventilation and air-conditioning systems, as specified in the EU Performance of Buildings Directive, is recommended, to ensure optimal performance in terms of environment, safety and energy-efficiency – independent assessment, and maintenance of HVAC equipment can limit the build-up of contaminants, helping to curb the indoor spread of infectious agents and improving overall air quality. This is of critical importance during building retrofits that involve tightening the building envelope, (by augmenting thermal insulation, for example), as it increases the need for well-maintained ventilation systems to prevent air stagnation and the potential deterioration of indoor air quality.  Finally, an academic review of multiple studies found that increased airtightness in retrofitted builds was associated with improvements in air quality, such as reduced infiltration of outdoor pollutants like PM2.5 and NO₂ in areas with high external pollution. These findings indicate that retrofitting actions which enhance the tightness of buildings may, under the right conditions, themselves help to improve indoor air quality. 
Outdoor Air Quality  Energy-efficient retrofitting and insulation measures are also capable of easing pressure on urban air quality. The more buildings meet, or exceed, stringent efficiency requirements, the less energy is consumed for heating and cooling, cutting urban CO₂ and pollutant emissions at source. Given that the European building stock accounts for 40% of energy use and is an important contributor to urban air pollution, compliance with building standards and retrofitting requirements, (and voluntary measures exceeding these,) offers an effective strategy for improving outdoor air quality.   Air quality and Infectious Disease Risk  Improving both indoor and outdoor air quality has important co-benefits for reducing infectious disease risk. Air quality is closely linked to human health, as pollution and other airborne contaminants and pathogens are known to aggravate a variety of respiratory conditions and diseases. Air pollution, particulate matter and contaminants such as radon can increase the risk of strokes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, and lower respiratory infections. (The interaction between air pollution and extreme heat is particularly harmful to those with pre-existing conditions). These conditions weaken overall resilience, increasing individual vulnerability to infectious diseases; cardiovascular and respiratory illnesses, for example, are known biological risk factors for vulnerability to vector-borne infections. 
 Furthermore, older or poorly ventilated buildings that fail to regulate indoor moisture can create humid conditions that promote mould growth, which fuels the development and exacerbation of respiratory diseases, such as asthma: these can significantly undermine overall resilience to infectious and respiratory illnesses.   Conversely, improving air quality can alleviate respiratory and cardiovascular stress, helping to reduce the prevalence and severity of these health conditions and thereby lowering human vulnerability to infectious disease.   Importantly, cleaner air can also lower the transmission rates of respiratory infections, both by decreasing the concentration of airborne particulates that act as carriers for pathogens, and by improving overall respiratory health and immune response. Consequently, improving indoor air quality is critical to protecting building occupants from the transmission of respiratory viruses and bacteria, particularly in enclosed, shared environments.  An example of an international building standard that addresses this transmission risk is ASHRAE Standard 241, Control of Infectious Aerosols, which establishes minimum requirements aimed at reducing the risk of disease transmission through exposure to airborne pathogens in buildings (whether new, existing or under major renovation). While it is a voluntary standard (unless adopted or referenced in legislation), it acknowledges that microscopic particles exhaled by individuals can carry disease-causing pathogens that, as seen during the Coronavirus pandemic, must be effectively filtered to prevent exposure among building occupants. Notably, its requirements largely focus on air system design and maintenance.  
Conclusively, retrofits, especially those emphasising ventilation and indoor environmental quality, can play a vital role in reducing both exposure and overall vulnerability to infectious diseases by improving indoor and outdoor air quality.
Through reduced emissions and minimised airborne pollutants, these measures can create healthier environments that support resilience to infectious diseases and limit their transmission. 

4.3.4. Trade-off of allergies & respiratory issues  While certain retrofitting measures can improve air quality – which is associated with better respiratory health – these interventions can also result in inadequate ventilation and total airtightness within the building envelope. Together, these conditions increase air stagnation, driving the accumulation of pollutants and pathogens in indoor environments. This deterioration in air quality can directly exacerbate asthma, allergic conditions, and respiratory infections in vulnerable individuals, while also increasing the transmission of respiratory viruses, thereby elevating overall susceptibility to infectious diseases.  A common assumption is that indoor air is cleaner than polluted outdoor air; however, research indicates that indoor air can often be more contaminated. Contributing factors include gases from cooking and heating, chemical residues from household cleaners, mould, mildew, and various toxins emitted by synthetic building materials. Furthermore, indoor use of pesticides, cleansers, paints and varnishes and air fresheners, further distribute toxins throughout the home. Consequently, proper ventilation of indoor spaces is critical to limiting the build-up of pollutants and protecting occupants’ health.   However, where retrofits introduce systems that merely heat or cool while recirculating, rather than replacing, air, such as certain ductless heat pumps, indoor air quality can deteriorate.   Moreover, the ‘tightening’ of the building envelope, often the central objective of energy-efficiency retrofitting (for example, through window replacements, or adding façade and roof insulation), can compound this issue by trapping polluted air indoors. This increases the concentration of harmful substances, presenting significant health risks. For example, post-retrofit studies have recorded elevated CO₂ and PM2.5 levels within homes, despite occupants reporting improved thermal comfort.  In fact, limited research has shown that, without proper ventilation systems, retrofitted buildings can exhibit higher radon levels than older, less-sealed structures . This is due to the presence of ‘leaky’ joints, floor gaps, and other natural ventilation pathways in older homes, suggesting that unrenovated buildings may offer unintended but beneficial air exchange, which retrofitted airtight builds lack.   Air stagnation, reduced ventilation, and the deterioration of air quality present serious challenges for infectious disease resilience, as air quality is intimately tied to human health. Airborne contaminants and pathogens are known to aggravate a variety of respiratory conditions and diseases: air pollution, particulate matter and contaminants such as radon can increase the risk of strokes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, and lower respiratory infections. (The interaction between air pollution and extreme heat is particularly harmful to those with pre-existing conditions). These conditions weaken overall resilience, increasing individual vulnerability to infectious diseases; cardiovascular and respiratory illnesses, for example, are known biological risk factors for vulnerability to vector-borne infections.   Indoor pollutants, such as dust, mould, pet dander and chemicals, typically more concentrated indoors than outside, can equally fuel the development and worsening of respiratory diseases. These illnesses weaken the immune system, making individuals generally more susceptible to foodborne, waterborne and vector-borne infectious diseases. Even insulation products themselves, such as spray polyurethane, can be a source of unwanted indoor emissions. This highlights the need not only to filter polluted outdoor air but also to effectively ventilate indoor air to prevent the accumulation of internally generated contaminants.  Furthermore, retrofitting measures that result in poor ventilation and fail to regulate indoor moisture can create damp, humid conditions that encourage mould growth – a known contaminant which fuels the development and exacerbation of respiratory diseases, such as asthma; these further undermine overall resilience to infectious and respiratory illnesses.   The presence of aeroallergens indoors can provoke allergic reactions, triggering airway irritation and inflammation of the respiratory tract, which may impair immunity of the mucosal system, reduce airway clearance, and compromise lung function, ultimately creating conditions that can facilitate bacterial or viral colonisation. Elevated systemic and local inflammation associated with allergic responses can further weaken the body’s ability to mount effective immune defences. In asthma, for example, underlying inflammation has been shown to impair antiviral immune responses, which would increase susceptibility to infections.   Indoor air pollution can also compound the effects of thermal stress, further exacerbating health impacts and the infectious disease vulnerability associated with higher temperatures (see 4.2.5.).  Consequently, integrating appropriate ventilation techniques – preferably mechanical ventilation systems which have been shown to improve air quality metrics – is essential when retrofitting in ways that insulate and increase airtightness. This is critical to preventing the accumulation of pollutants, allergens, and contaminants, and mitigating their negative impacts on human health, respiratory conditions, and broader vulnerability to infectious diseases.   (It should be observed that an academic review of 20 studies found no consistent negative correlation between increased airtightness and indoor air quality across various parameters. In some instances, greater airtightness was associated with improvements, such as reduced infiltration of outdoor pollutants like PM2.5 and NO₂ in areas with high external pollution. These findings indicate that certain retrofitting actions which enhance the tightness of buildings may, under the right conditions, help to improve, rather than compromise, indoor air quality.)

4.3.5. Trade-off of overheating
 Certain retrofitting actions, particularly those aimed at enhancing thermal insulation, can increase the airtightness of the building envelope, inadvertently contributing to overheating. This presents significant risks for human health and can heighten vulnerability to infectious diseases.  Although improving energy efficiency is often the primary goal of retrofits, research has shown that increased insulation, which traps heat within buildings, can lead to overheating under future climate conditions. Counterintuitively, in the absence of active cooling, older residential buildings may remain cooler than newer builds in future climates, owing to their greater air leakage, which allows excess heat to dissipate more steadily.  Without the integration of mechanical or even passive cooling strategies – both of which have demonstrated effectiveness under current and projected climatic conditions – certain retrofit measures risk exacerbating indoor overheating, which in turn can undermine occupant health and resilience to infectious disease.   Excessive indoor heat can trigger a range of immediate heat-related illnesses, such as heat exhaustion, heat stroke, and dehydration. Beyond acute illness, sustained exposure to high temperatures can increase inflammation, worsen pre-existing chronic health conditions, mental health, and place added strain on cardiovascular and respiratory systems. These physiological burdens weaken immune function, rendering individuals more susceptible to infectious disease. Excessive heat and heat-related illnesses are equally associated with an increase in mortality, thus reducing overall public health resilience during disease outbreaks.  Consequently, retrofitting measures that significantly increase thermal insulation, without accounting for future climate projections and incorporating appropriate cooling strategies, risk inducing overheating in indoor spaces, with detrimental consequences for individual vulnerability to infectious disease and broader public health outcomes.   
4.3.6. Mitigation Potential 

Retrofitting and insulation measures can equally be powerful tools for climate mitigation: buildings play a critical role, not only in adapting to changing climate conditions but also in reducing greenhouse gas emissions. The European Union has set ambitious targets to support this, mandating that by 2030, buildings’ greenhouse gas emissions must be cut by 60%, final energy consumption reduced by 14%, and energy use for heating and cooling decreased by 18% to meet the overall 55% emission reduction goal. This urgency stems from the fact that buildings are responsible for approximately 40% of the EU’s total energy consumption and 36% of its energy-related greenhouse gas emissions. Consequently, retrofitting and insulation measures that effectively improve the energy efficiency of buildings and reduce energy consumption are critically important for reducing national emissions and meeting climate mitigation goals. 

Climate change is primarily caused by the accumulation of greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide and methane, in the atmosphere, which trap heat and disrupt global climate systems. Reducing these emissions slows the pace of global warming, helping to prevent the most severe impacts on ecosystems, human health, and economies. Therefore, reducing greenhouse gas emissions is the fundamental strategy for mitigating climate change. Energy efficiency measures, including retrofitting and insulation strategies, thus offer an effective, targeted approach to climate change mitigation.
