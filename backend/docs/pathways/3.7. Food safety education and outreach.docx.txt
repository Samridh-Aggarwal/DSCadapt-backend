3.7. Food safety education and outreach
 Education and outreach are vital to food safety. They can take many forms, both public and private: mandatory workplace training, government public‐service announcements, school curricula, social programmes, and company-wide initiatives within the food sector.  The European Union strongly advocates for educational and outreach initiatives, aiming to uphold a high level of protection for human health and consumer interests. The 2021 FoodSafety4EU project exemplifies this approach, recognising risk communication as integral to effective food safety governance. The project sought to increase engagement through a multi-stakeholder platform that enables food safety authorities, consumer associations, academic institutions, research centres, think tanks, and food industry representatives to share information and refine risk communication models, tailoring them to different target audiences. Similarly, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) emphasises risk communication as a vital component of food safety policy and strives to expand its outreach efforts to improve understanding and trust with customers, partners, and the public at large.   3.7.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change
 Climate change is expected to profoundly disrupt food systems, influencing not only the production of food but also its distribution, quality and safety. Changes in soil fertility, increasing temperatures and the frequency of extreme weather events such as floods and droughts threaten agricultural productivity, reducing yields and constraining the affordability and availability of food. Critically, extreme weather events can directly compromise food safety: flooding, heavy rainfall and hurricanes can facilitate the spread of bacterial and viral contaminants throughout the food chain, as floodwaters containing sewage, animal waste and agricultural run-off provide ideal conditions for the proliferation of waterborne pathogens, including norovirus, E. coli, Giardiasis, Hepatitis A and Campylobacteriosis.   Rising ambient temperatures and increased humidity further jeopardise food safety by stimulating greater microbial activity within food systems. Foodborne pathogens such as Salmonella, E. coli, and listeria demonstrate accelerated growth and longer survival in warmer environments, increasing the speed and scale at which food contamination can occur. Collectively, climate-induced changes influence the abundance, growth, geographic range and survival of foodborne pathogens, elevating risks across all stages of the food chain, from processing and transport to storage and consumption, and amplifying the likelihood of outbreaks of foodborne illness.   Additionally, extreme heat and severe weather can undermine the stability of infrastructure critical to maintaining food safety. Power outages or equipment failures during heatwaves, storms or flooding can disrupt and disable refrigeration systems and cold storage facilities, leading to food spoilage and heightening the risk of contamination. Coastal flooding and storm surges can inflict further damage on storage facilities and distribution networks. For instance, following Hurricane Sandy in 2012, disruptions to refrigeration systems caused an outbreak of salmonellosis in the north-eastern United States. Higher temperatures, increased humidity and rising sea levels also place stress on food system infrastructure, including machinery, transport and telecommunications, which collectively create vulnerabilities throughout the food chain. An example includes the deterioration of livestock feed quality under warmer and more humid conditions, resulting in increased post-harvest losses at the farm level and potentially driving the growth of harmful moulds and bacteria.   Broadly, climate change-driven decreases in biodiversity and alterations to ecosystems further increase the risk of zoonotic disease emergence: habitat disruption and shifts in species distribution can increase contact between wildlife and livestock, parasite transmission and heightening the possibility of zoonotic spillover. As witnessed during the COVID-19 pandemic, zoonotic pathogens can persist in domesticated animals and cross species barriers, with severe implications for global health.  
 Given these interconnected risks, it is crucial that both private and public actors across the food chain are equipped to recognise and respond effectively to these challenges. Educating consumers, food handlers, and producers about the heightened risks of foodborne pathogens, particularly under specific climatic conditions, and the measures available to prevent contamination is essential to enhancing the resilience of the food system. Strengthening and updating food safety education and outreach to reflect changing patterns of contamination and disease proliferation can help ensure that, across the food chain and especially at the point of consumption, individuals can apply safe practices that mitigate infection risks. Both public and private initiatives that provide science-based and accurate information, such as awareness campaigns, targeted training and extension services for producers and processors, collectively contribute to this vital adaptation objective. 
3.7.2. Adaptation measures  Research shows that targeted interventions and customised educational programs can be highly effective in bolstering community resilience to climate-driven foodborne risks. Such initiatives should include information on particular pathogens, risks that rise under specific combinations of temperature and humidity, correct practices for handling, storage, cooking and monitoring, and how these risks shift with seasonal or extreme weather variations. More generally, outreach and education are vital in helping the public understand how climate change alters the persistence, dispersal and severity of foodborne pathogens. Public health decision makers are therefore encouraged to education and outreach interventions as preventive adaptation tools.    The following list includes examples of climate resilient food safety education and outreach measures:  
	•	School programs: The European SchoolFood4Change project engages over 3000 schools and 600,000 pupils across 12 countries, focusing on community-wide capacity building to aid the shift to sustainable and healthy diets. Incorporating food safety information during project expansion could be highly effective in combating foodborne disease risk. 
	•	Capacity-building workshops: On-site training for key stakeholders, such as caterers, kitchen staff, farmers, local public health officials can strengthen food chain actors’ response to climate-related risks. The SchoolFood4Change project conducts on-site trainings throughout Europe.
	•	Educational toolkits: The dissemination of food safety information through accessible online resources—particularly multimedia and interactive materials – can enhance stakeholder literacy in sustainable and safe food consumption within a changing climate. Educational toolkits should be designed for diverse audiences and age groups, enabling a variety of facilitators to use them effectively. 
	•	Public awareness and communication campaigns:  Tools employed, such as those in the EU Food Safety Platform’s work, include PSAs, digital posters, edutainment via social media, interactive workshops, and glossaries in multiple languages. Such tools can target specific food items that carry elevated risks, such as oysters, raw meat and unpasteurised milk, consumption of which, though rare, can transmit Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever and tick-borne encephalitis. 
	•	Training for health professionals and food inspectors: Improving training for food safety inspectors, laboratory analysts, health care workers and extension services so they can share information, identify and respond to climate-exacerbated risks can enhance food safety across public and private sectors and strengthen early warning and surveillance.  
	•	Labelling, guidelines & consumer information tools: Introducing warning labels or advisory guidance on foods that are more vulnerable to spoilage or contamination can support safe cooking, cooling and storage practices especially in periods of elevated risk. 
	•	Extension and support services for producers and processors:  Delivering education and advisory services on the augmented risks of foodborne pathogens to both small-scale and industrial food producers can reduce the risk of contamination and spoilage during farming, harvesting, post-harvest and processing. 
	•	Governance and local authority training:
Ensuring that officials responsible for food control, surveillance and safety have specific training in climate-related foodborne disease risks can improve their capacity to monitor risks and enforce effective adaptation measures.    
3.7.3. (Co-)benefit of improved food safety    Strengthening and updating food safety education and outreach measures to incorporate climate resilience objectives and address the growing risks posed by foodborne pathogens can help reduce contamination and limit spread of infectious diseases. Expanding and increasing the dissemination of food safety education to reflect the heightened threats linked to specific weather patterns, seasonal variations, and climate change more broadly can reduce the emergence and proliferation of pathogens responsible for these illnesses. In doing so, these measures contribute to improved food safety and the protection of public health.  Collectively, food safety education and outreach cover a broad spectrum of foodborne pathogens. As such, their improvement offers wide-ranging benefits, effectively reducing the risk of numerous infectious diseases sensitive to climate change, whether exacerbated by higher temperatures, increased humidity, weather-driven contamination or general damage to food system infrastructure. These include bacterial, viral, and parasitic infections such as Campylobacteriosis, Cryptosporidiosis, E. coli, Giardiasis, Hepatitis A, Listeriosis, Norovirus, Salmonellosis, Shigellosis, and Vibriosis.  As these diseases are influenced by changing climatic conditions, strengthening and updating food safety education and outreach initiatives as part of climate adaptation strategies builds sector-wide resilience while also directly improving food safety and reducing the overall risks of infectious foodborne illnesses.
