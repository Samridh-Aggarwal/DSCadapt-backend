3.3. Protect and restore biodiversity, habitats and natural resources   3.3.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change

Biodiversity represents the extraordinary variety of life on Earth and is fundamental to sustaining human existence. At genetic, species, and ecosystem level, biodiversity underpins our food system, providing fundamental ecosystem services including pollination, pest control, soil formation and nutrient cycling, carbon sequestration and oxygen production, and purifying and regulating our water supplies; biodiversity even reduces disaster threats, increasing resilience to economic and climate shocks and stresses, and providing habitat for beneficial species. However, biodiversity is critically threatened – by overexploitation, pollution, land and sea use changes, invasive alien species, and importantly, climate change.  Climate change poses a severe threat to global biodiversity and ecosystems by altering habitats, shifting species distributions, and disrupting ecological processes. Rising temperatures, ocean acidification, and the increasing frequency and intensity of extreme weather events such as droughts, floods, and wildfires lead to habitat degradation or complete (sometimes, irreversible) loss, and threaten existing food webs, causing species populations to become increasingly isolated or vulnerable to extinction.   Climate change is equally projected to alter the life cycles of plants and animals. For example, higher temperatures are expected to cause many plants to grow and flower earlier in spring, while extending the growing season into autumn. Similarly, some animals may emerge from hibernation sooner or adjust their migration patterns. These changes have cascading effects on ecosystems and the essential services they provide to society. Climate change-induced species invasions pose an additional threat, capable of eroding native biodiversity and destabilising ecosystems.   Indeed, the biodiversity crisis and the climate crisis are intrinsically linked: as climate change accelerates the destruction of natural ecosystems through extreme weather and ecological disruption, the loss and unsustainable use of biodiversity and natural resources, (such as deforestation and soil degradation,) are, in turn, key drivers of climate change.   As of 2020, only 15% of European habitats were in good conservation status, while 81% were in poor or bad condition, marking a deterioration from the previous reporting period. At the EU level, only a quarter of species exhibit a good conservation status; reptiles, vascular plants, and certain fish species, for instance, demonstrate strong or improving trends, while bird populations and wild pollinators continue to decline.   Biodiversity loss and ecosystem collapse are considered by the EU to be among the greatest threats facing humanity in the coming decade; they jeopardise the foundations of our economy, with the costs of inaction projected to rise significantly. For instance, the consequences of biodiversity loss will include reduced crop yields and fish catches, increased economic losses from flooding and other disasters, and the diminished potential for discovering new, vital sources of medicine.    Consequently, the protection and restoration of biodiversity, habitats and natural resources, including forests, wetlands, freshwater systems, soils, and marine habitats, is a major priority for both adapting to climate change and mitigating its effects. Improving conservation statuses and alleviating pressures on biodiversity from agriculture, urbanisation, forestry, and pollution, (among other concerns) are essential to reversing habitat and species decline and safeguarding the critical ecosystem services that biodiversity provides.  
At European level, the EU Biodiversity Strategy for 2030, (part of the wider European Green Deal), is the central piece of relevant legislation, emphasising the fundamental economic, sociocultural, and ecological importance of biodiversity protection and recovery. It emphasises nature-based solutions and Natura 2000, a network of protected areas covering Europe's most valuable and threatened species and habitats, as critical actions for biodiversity support and climate adaptation. Additionally, the Nature Restoration regulation sets binding targets for member states to restore degraded ecosystems, aiming to recover EU’s biodiversity and increase the resilience of ecosystems, habitats and species to natural disasters and climate change threats. Other relevant examples of legislation include the EU Strategy on Green Infrastructure, which promotes ecosystem-based approaches to adapt infrastructure, such as restoring wetlands to reduce flood risks while also improving biodiversity, and the EU Forest Strategy for 2030, which aims to combat biodiversity loss by growing healthy, diverse, and resilient EU forests.   At national level, several examples illustrate this approach to biodiversity and ecosystem protection. The Spanish National Climate Change Adaptation Plan includes measures to assess the impacts of climate change on local flora, fauna and ecosystem function, and to mitigate non-climate pressures and reduce stress levels for species and ecosystems, to help maintain and improve biodiversity resilience. The German National Adaptation Action Plan focuses on optimising habitats for climate-sensitive and endangered species to support their adaptation; actions include maintaining sufficiently large areas with habitat protection functions and promoting greater ‘naturalness’ of ecosystems.   
3.3.2. Adaptation measures

	•	Ecosystem restoration: This broad term means assisting in the recovery of ecosystems that have been degraded or destroyed, and natural interventions to conserve threatened ecosystems. Restoration can occur directly, e.g., through active planting, and indirectly, by removing external pressures to allow nature to recover. There is, however, debate surrounding the definition of restoration, and ‘total’ recovery of an ecosystem is not always possible; shifting baseline syndrome, and insufficient information about historical baselines can also prevent or weaken restoration efforts. In certain cases, restoration may be undesirable, as farmland or infrastructure on land that once was forest are now necessary to support societies. 
	•	Reforestation/forest restoration: Reforestation, in particular with climate-resilient native tree species, is essential to help to combat the effects of climate change and biodiversity loss, helping to stabilise soils, contribute to natural cycles, and provide habitat for wildlife. 
	•	Construction of ecological corridors/green networks: Corridors facilitate species migration and genetic exchange under changing climatic conditions, supporting biodiversity by augmenting and enhancing the connectivity of safe habitats for wildlife. 
	•	Establishing and increasing protected areas: Protected areas are clearly defined geographical spaces that are recognised as and dedicated to achieving the long-term conservation of nature. The EU’s vast network of protected areas is known as Natura 2000, representing 27,000 protected sites across the EU, and the largest network globally. These areas cover both land and sea, with various (at times disputed) levels of protection and effectiveness. Not all protected areas fall under the Natura 2000 network; for instance, some national parks and strictly protected reserves are designated as such under national legislation, even though they may benefit from comparable protection. 
	•	Assisted migration or translocation of vulnerable species to suitable habitats
	•	Conservation of genetic diversity: This can be achieved both through natural interventions, like habitat loss prevention, and through more technical measures, such as seed banks, gene banks and ex situ conservation. 
	•	Improving water retention in natural landscapes: Improving water retention can help regulate water cycles, replenish groundwater and support biodiversity in water scarce areas. 
	•	Soil conservation measures: These include erosion control, certain sustainable agriculture practices (further details can be found in 3.1. Sustainable Farming Strategies), pollution and contamination prevention, and organic matter enhancement. 
	•	Controlling invasive alien species  Invasive alien species threaten biodiversity by outcompeting native species, altering habitats, disrupting ecosystem functions, and introducing diseases. Control can occur in the form of prevention measures to restrict their introduction, early detection and rapid eradication of new invasions, and long-term management to contain established populations. 
	•	Preventing habitat loss, e.g., banning developments in certain areas. 
	•	Reducing pollution in ecologically sensitive and nature-adjacent areas: Examples include motor vehicle restrictions and regulations limiting agrochemical use near protected areas.
	•	Agroforestry systems: Agroforestry systems are a sustainable land-use management practice that integrates trees and natural elements, such as hedges, into farmland, to enhance biodiversity, improve soil health, and increase farm productivity. (Further details can be found in 3.1. Sustainable Farming Strategies). 
	•	Sustainable forest management: Integrating ecological, biodiversity, and climate resilience considerations into forest management and related industries, such as logging. 
	•	Wildfire prevention measures: Though some practices are controversial, measures such as controlled burns and firebreaks can successfully limit wildfires and prevent widescale biodiversity loss in fire-prone areas. Awareness raising of fire risks equally reduces the potential for human-induced wildfires. 
	•	Vulnerability assessments for biodiversity under climate change: As specified in the Spanish National Adaptation Plan, these assessments can help direct protective resources towards the most threatened habitats and species. 
	•	Species conservation measures 
	•	Urban nature-based solutions: Green and blue spaces in urban environments can enhance biodiversity, provide relief from extreme weather events, and improve overall habitat connectivity. This is particularly important given that urban areas can, in some cases, support greater species richness than conventionally farmed rural landscapes (see Section 4.1 for further details). 

3.3.3. Co-benefit of providing healthy ecosystems

The protection and restoration of biodiversity, habitats and natural resources can offer the added co-benefit of supporting infectious disease resilience by contributing to the overall health and stability of ecosystems.    Biodiversity and ecosystem health are both associated with greater resilience to infectious disease. In degraded ecosystems and areas of weakened genetic and species diversity, the complexity and heterogeneity of the natural system are compromised, which makes it more vulnerable and at a heightened risk of creating new opportunities for disease emergence. Notably, the risk of infectious zoonotic diseases is often linked to the degradation or destruction of ecosystems; research further specifies that the loss of pre-existing biodiversity is strongly associated with substantial increases in infectious disease outcomes.  Conversely, more biodiverse environments are widely understood to be more resistant to disease emergence due to the ‘dilution effect,’ a phenomenon in which diverse ecosystems can offer pathogens a greater variety of hosts, some of which are less competent reservoirs or less susceptible to disease. This reduces the likelihood of successful transmission, thereby diluting opportunities for the pathogen to thrive. Consequently, mitigating biodiversity loss, restoring natural habitats, and strengthening ecosystem health can help control the presence and spread of disease vectors, thereby reducing the risk of infectious disease. Constructed wetlands, for example, have been shown to help mitigate antimicrobial resistance (AMR) – a serious threat to public health as it can render standard treatments for infectious diseases ineffective – by limiting the dispersal of pathogens and antibiotic-resistant bacteria in riverine systems.   When it comes to biodiversity and habitat protection and restoration, enhancing connectivity between natural areas can be particularly effective for supporting biodiversity. Ecological corridors and urban greenways aim to reverse habitat fragmentation, creating connections between forested and natural areas, hedgerows, trees, parks, and any other green and blue spaces. In doing so, these corridors offer continuous refuge and mobility, maximising biodiversity protection and support. This strengthens ecosystem health, which, in turn, helps to reduce the risk of infectious diseases. A New York study of vertebrate diversity and disease transmission illustrates this relationship, in which forest fragmentation was found to decrease the number of different vertebrate species, allowing white-footed mice – highly competent hosts for Lyme-disease-carrying ticks – to dominate, elevating the overall Lyme risk for nearby humans. Conversely, in more diverse forests, ticks were also diverted towards less competent hosts like squirrels and birds, which diluted opportunities for disease transmission. Restoring habitat connectivity is especially critical for maintaining pollinator diversity, which helps ensure reliable pollination and, in turn, sustains plant communities, wildlife, and overall ecosystem health.  
Consequently, protecting and, importantly, restoring biodiversity, habitats, and natural resources is critical for reducing the prevalence and transmission of certain infectious pathogens and for enhancing overall resilience to infectious disease.  
3.3.4. Co-benefit of safe and clean water resources 

The protection and restoration of biodiversity, habitats and natural resources – particularly freshwater ecosystems – offers a crucial co-benefit for infectious disease risk by supporting the availability of safe and clean water.  Well-functioning ecosystems play a vital role in the water cycle, storing, filtering, and purifying water, regulating flow, protecting from drought, and mitigating floods, which otherwise increase the risks of contamination from storm runoff and sewage overflow. Biodiversity strongly influences water cycles in wetlands, streams and aquifers, through processes such as energy exchange, and evapotranspiration from trees and plants; indeed, research demonstrates that functional biodiversity in forests directly correlates with improved water regulation, helping to moderate flows, prevent floods and maintain water availability.    Additionally, biodiversity is closely linked to water quality.  Forests and connected vegetation purify water by slowing runoff, trapping organic matter, and filtering sediments and pollutants through root systems. Marshes, riparian buffers, and aquatic plants have also been shown to absorb harmful compounds such as ammonia, nitrates, and phosphorus. This natural filtration contributes to overall water quality – in contrast, deforestation is associated with the loss of this ecosystem service, and reduced water quality. Indeed, higher forest densities near waterfalls have been shown to strengthen water purification.   Protecting or restoring biodiversity and habitats thus benefits the availability of safe and clean water resources; as flora and fauna themselves rely on water resources, restoration efforts often explicitly address water systems, for example, by removing waste from rivers, reducing pollution inputs, conserving wetlands that support groundwater recharge, and managing water extraction sustainably in protected areas.     By safeguarding clean water resources, biodiversity and habitat protection ultimately reduce infectious disease risk: safe, potable water is fundamental not only for human survival, but equally for essential hygiene practices that limit disease transmission. Maintaining the availability of clean water thus supports public health, preventing the consumption of low-quality water and reducing outbreaks of infectious waterborne diseases linked to contaminated sources. 

Supporting this, areas with greater vegetation diversity have been shown to promote microbial communities that help break down contaminants, eliminating pathogens such as Escherichia coli (E. coli), a major cause of waterborne illnesses. Furthermore, forest restoration, particularly in riparian zones, has been shown to effectively protect water bodies from pollutants, increasing water quality, supporting safeguarding freshwater resources and reducing risks to human health. 


3.3.5. Co-benefit of enhanced mental health & physical activity
 Protecting and restoring biodiversity, habitats and natural resources can play a meaningful role in improving mental and physical health, and, in turn, lowering both individual and collective vulnerability to infectious disease risks.   Natural environments and green and blue spaces are widely recognised for their capacity to offer considerable health and wellbeing benefits – living near, spending recreational time in, and feeling psychologically connected to nature are consistently associated with improved mental health outcomes.   These benefits are thought to arise through mechanisms such as increased social interaction, cognitive restoration, reduced stress, and psychological recovery. Research has shown that densely urbanised areas with limited access to green and blue spaces are linked to higher risks of psychosis and depression in both women and men. Greener cities have been associated with long-term improvements in mental health, highlighting the potential of urban biodiversity and restoration policies to deliver sustained public health gains, and blue spaces, whether oceans, beaches, lakes or rivers, are also increasingly recognised for their wellbeing benefits, with both visual exposure and physical visits shown to positively influence mental health. Nature connectedness more broadly is positively associated with wellbeing and negatively associated with psychological distress – visiting natural environments has even been linked to a reduced likelihood of using medication for depression.   When protection and restoration efforts increase opportunities for connection with nature, whether by maintaining the availability of natural spaces or recovering accessibility to natural spaces, they are thus accompanied by these additional wellbeing benefits.   In urban and peri-urban areas, green and blue spaces can equally contribute to a more tranquil environment by buffering noise – plants are particularly efficient at doing so, and research shows that green walls and green roofs, and even hedges, exhibit strong potential for sound insulation in buildings. Chronic or sudden exposure to noise, particularly from traffic, can adversely impact the central nervous system, increasing the susceptibility to mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, suicide, and increasing the risks of cardiological problems and stroke, impaired cognitive performance, and even behavioural problems in both children and adolescents. Implementing green and blue infrastructure to reduce and distance noise can therefore meaningfully improve mental and emotional wellbeing, both indoors and outdoors. 
The same applies to heat management, as vegetation and natural environments help cool temperatures, providing relief and reducing both immediate and long-term health risks associated with prolonged heat exposure (see 4.1.4. Co-benefit of heat management). Cities can be 10–15°C hotter than surrounding rural areas, a growing problem that can be mitigated by restoring urban green and blue spaces.  Furthermore, natural environments and green and blue spaces typically offer cleaner, purified air, reduced traffic, and open areas, providing opportunities for outdoor recreation and physical activity, both of which are strongly linked to improved health and wellbeing and reduced rates of depression.   The health benefits of increased contact with diverse ecosystems and natural green and blue spaces may even extend to long-term effects associated with human exposure to greater microbial diversity. For instance, research shows that the risk of developing asthma is lower in children living on traditional farmland, an environment rich in microbial variety. This has led to the hypothesis that similar benefits may result from exposure to the diverse microbial communities present in biodiverse natural environments, as these microbes can transfer from soil and air to the human gut, supporting a range of health and wellbeing outcomes. 

Conversely, ecological degradation is linked to numerous public health challenges, including pollution, water contamination, poor mental health, and heightened vulnerability to extreme weather events. Restoring degraded rural areas has been shown to deliver significant health and wellbeing benefits, not only through improved environmental conditions, but equally through active participation in restoration activities. 
 In general, nature can provide opportunities for recreation, exercise, and learning, as well as nurturing a sense of place and aesthetic value, all of which can decisively contribute to better health and wellbeing. As climate change is projected to worsen physical and mental outcomes worldwide, particularly among vulnerable individuals and communities – due to heat stress, air pollution, increased infectious disease risk, pressure on health systems, and by extreme weather events causing PTSD, anxiety, and depression – protecting and restoring natural ecosystems represents a meaningful and effective strategy to mitigate the growing burden on physical health, mental health and overall wellbeing. 

In supporting mental and physical health and promoting physical activity, these measures offer key benefits for strengthening resilience against infectious diseases. As evidenced, access to healthy natural environments and green and blue spaces is consistently linked to reduced stress, anxiety, and depression; by buffering environmental stressors like noise and pollution they also enhance psychological, physical, and respiratory health. Restoring natural environments not only builds resilience against life-threatening events like flooding, but also encourages outdoor activities and regular physical exercise, which are associated with improved immune response, mental health, and cardiovascular function, among other benefits. 
 Ultimately, the varied health and wellbeing benefits offered by natural areas are closely linked to stronger immune function, which directly reduces individual vulnerability to infectious disease. Sudden or chronic stress and mental health conditions can impair immune function, reducing the body’s ability to combat pathogens and potentially leading to more severe disease outcomes; chronic illnesses such as cardiovascular and respiratory diseases also compromise immune resilience.   Consequently, protecting and restoring biodiversity, habitats, and natural resources in ways that enhance their accessibility and support their health benefits can offer important improvements for both mental and physical health, thereby lowering both individual and collective vulnerability to infectious disease risks. 

3.3.6. Trade-off of ecological disruption

By altering natural ecosystems, certain biodiversity and habitat protection or restoration efforts may carry the trade-off of increased infectious disease risk through ecological disruption.   Biodiversity and habitat protection and restoration projects, while largely intended to enhance ecosystem services, can unintentionally disrupt others. This risk is heightened when projects target single species or services in isolation. Additionally, projects that are slow or halted before completion can leave ecosystems in an altered, suboptimal state.  In doing so, such interventions can disturb existing infectious disease dynamics – sometimes positively, as discussed in 3.3.3. Co-benefit of Healthy Ecosystems – but also in ways that inadvertently exacerbate disease risk.  When interventions in biodiversity, habitats, or natural resources are myopically designed, poorly implemented, or inadequately monitored, they may alter natural processes, shift resource availability, and change how people interact with an ecosystem; these changes can affect species composition and abundance, ultimately influencing disease ecology.   This is because biodiverse environments may help suppress certain pathogens, yet they may, at times, favour their abundance. Indeed, the scientific community has long debated whether biodiversity mitigates or amplifies infectious disease risk, as vector-borne pathogens, generalist wildlife, and zoonotic pathogens are particularly sensitive to biodiversity changes. Specifically, studies evidence mixed relationships between biodiversity and disease, often dependent on scale: at large scales, biodiversity correlates positively with disease burden, but this association weakens over time; at smaller scales biodiversity tends to reduce disease risk, a phenomenon known as the ‘dilution effect’ (as discussed in 3.3.3. Co-benefit of healthy ecosystems). Ultimately, research asserts that biodiversity and protecting habitats can indeed reduce zoonotic disease spillover risk, but in some cases, targeted species or habitat management and biomedical interventions may be more effective.   Consequently, if biodiversity, habitat, and natural resource protection or restoration efforts are undertaken without accounting for infectious disease risks, or without integrating appropriate disease control measures, they may disrupt existing ecological balances in ways that increase, rather than reduce, the likelihood of infectious disease emergence and prevalence, thereby posing risks to both human and wildlife health.

However, evidence suggests the dilution effect is stronger when biodiversity loss results from human disturbance, but weaker where biodiversity changes naturally. This implies that restoration efforts, perhaps even more so than protection, are crucial to ensuring that interventions deliver co-benefits rather than trade-offs for infectious disease risk.   

3.3.7. Trade-off of increased human presence in natural areas

Biodiversity and habitat protection and restoration projects may result, intentionally or otherwise, in increased human activity in a given natural area. Such activity can disturb wildlife and heighten human-wildlife interactions, potentially escalating disease transmission and thus presenting a trade-off for infectious disease risk.    Natural environments harbour both pathogens and their vectors, spanning numerous vertebrates, invertebrates and even bird species, that can transmit infectious diseases to humans. For example, birds can serve as hosts for West Nile virus, avian influenza, and tick-borne pathogens. If a natural area lies along the flight path of migratory birds, (often a deliberate feature in biodiversity support projects), the risk of zoonotic disease transmission may be heightened, as migratory birds are known to carry particularly high pathogen loads. Small mammals too, such as voles and mice, are carriers of hantavirus and leptospirosis, and serve as key hosts for tick-borne diseases. Invertebrates, most importantly, mosquitoes, ticks, and flies, act as primary vectors of illnesses including Lyme disease, tick-borne encephalitis, malaria, leishmaniasis, dengue, chikungunya, and Zika virus, all of which pose significant health risks to humans.   It is widely accepted that natural areas tend to harbour higher concentrations of wildlife and, consequently, greater densities of disease vectors – mosquitoes, for instance, are well documented to exhibit greater abundance and species richness in rural and natural areas compared to urban environments. Consequently, where biodiversity conservation and habitat restoration measures lead to increased human presence in such areas, opportunities for disease transmission, and overall exposure to infectious pathogens are likely to rise.   To mitigate this trade-off, implementing disease monitoring measures alongside awareness campaigns can effectively reduce these risks; clear health warnings and educational materials, for instance, should be prominently displayed near natural sites to ensure that visitors take appropriate precautions against (geographically relevant) diseases associated with increased wildlife interactions. 


3.3.8. Trade-off of land use conflict

Biodiversity protection and restoration can, at times, prove contentious, as these projects may not guarantee benefits for local livelihoods or address social justice concerns. When projects aiming to protect and restore biodiversity, habitats and natural resources conflict with alternative land uses, this may exacerbate social and economic tensions, thereby heightening vulnerability to infectious diseases.  Significant land use conflicts can arise when large areas, set aside for habitat protection and restoration or development of natural ecosystems, compete with alternative economic uses, such as agriculture or urban expansion. During periods of financial and social hardship – marked by unemployment, housing crises, food shortages, concerns over farmers’ rights, (or fishermen’s rights, in the case of marine protected areas), and other broader pressures associated with economic stagnation – the tension between extractivist or growth-oriented development and ecosystem restoration can fuel social discord.  

This is clearly illustrated by the debate over England’s Green Belt, where the need to build new homes to alleviate a severe housing crisis clashes with efforts to preserve rural areas. The conflict is vividly encapsulated in Prime Minister Keir Starmer’s assertion that ‘homes must have a higher priority than nature and the environment’. 

A further example arose in September 2021 in Marseille, France, where a coalition of agrarian and environmental advocates protested the International Union for the Conservation of Nature’s (IUCN) move to raise the global protected-area target from 16% (at the time) to 30% by 2030. In contrast, Indigenous People’s Organisation members submitted an opposing motion, urging protection of 80% of the Amazon rainforest by 2025, to defend their land rights.   Notoriously, protection of natural ecosystems can even conflict with the alternative environmental cause of Net-Zero emissions, as the restoration of land likely impedes its use for solar and wind energy installations. 

In terms of disease, land-use conflict tends to produce divisions and economic tensions, which can fuel social unrest and associated mental health concerns among affected populations; these factors carry consequences for infectious disease risk. Political protests, for instance, can increase exposure to disease by creating crowding at public demonstrations – these conditions increase the risk of transmission, as population density facilitates the spread of gastrointestinal and respiratory infections. Moreover, social unrest can trigger or exacerbate stress, anxiety, depression and other mental health conditions. These impacts are not only harmful in themselves but also reduce human resilience to disease, as poor mental health can impair immune function, reducing the body’s ability to combat pathogens and potentially leading to more severe disease outcomes.

An illustrative example of this trade-off relates to rural hospital provision. In certain European regions, rural hospitals are sparse; constructing a major healthcare facility in a natural area would bolster public health, potentially reducing vulnerability to infectious disease, yet plans for construction could conflict with an area’s protected status. Similarly, road infrastructure that improves access to hospitals and pharmacies could equally conflict with conservation or restoration projects. 

Furthermore, if the imposition of protected areas curtails job opportunities, such as through reduced fishing limits, this can diminish income for certain communities; in accordance with a One Health understanding, economic hardship and poverty is often associated with an increase in vulnerability to infectious disease.  

Consequently, while biodiversity and habitat protection and restoration remain vital measures for climate adaptation, where they generate or intensify land-use conflict that leads to both direct exposure risks and associated mental and physical health challenges, they carry a trade-off for infectious disease risk.    To mitigate this trade-off, participatory and inclusive strategies, such as those that integrate local stakeholders, are considered effective. By incorporating diverse perspectives and expertise during the planning and implementation of conservation and restoration projects, these approaches can alleviate tensions and help ensure that outcomes align with broader aims of climate justice and the reduction of socioeconomic inequalities.    

3.3.9. Mitigation Potential 
 Protecting and, most critically, restoring biodiversity, habitats, and natural resources can also serve as a powerful tool for climate mitigation. Healthy, biodiverse ecosystems reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) concentrations by capturing and storing carbon, mostly in the soil, with smaller amounts in trees and plants. (The amount of carbon stored depends on factors like tree age, light availability, and carbon present in the air, with younger trees demonstrating a tendency to absorb higher amounts). Consequently, protecting biodiverse, vegetated areas and natural environments can help maintain their capacity to sequester carbon – restoration measures may even enhance this function, increasing carbon sequestration and contributing to lower atmospheric GHG concentrations.  
 This is critically important, as climate change is primarily caused by the accumulation of greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide and methane, in the atmosphere, which trap heat and disrupt global climate systems. Absorbing these gases slows the pace of global warming, helping to prevent the most severe impacts on ecosystems, human health, and economies. Therefore, decreasing greenhouse gas concentrations – whether directly through reductions, or indirectly through absorption – is the fundamental strategy for mitigating climate change.   The relationship between biodiversity and carbon sequestration is well established. Hundreds of research studies have consistently shown that greater species diversity, particularly among plants, tends to increase biomass production and carbon sequestration. This may be because species with varied traits and resource needs can use ecosystem resources more efficiently, reducing competition and enhancing facilitation. More diverse areas are also more likely to include highly productive species, boosting overall ecosystem performance.  As outlined earlier, biodiversity loss is a key driver of ecosystem degradation and productivity decline. Since productive, healthy ecosystems are vital for carbon sequestration and storage, biodiversity loss can undermine climate mitigation efforts by disrupting these natural services.  
Research also indicates that landscape richness can improve the carbon sink capacity of green spaces, as higher biodiversity allows more ecological niches to be occupied, driving plant productivity. Conversely, the smaller and more fragmented natural areas are, the lower their carbon sink potential, highlighting the value of restorative actions that enhance ecosystem connectivity.   Concerningly, wildfires in natural areas risk reversing these ecosystem functions, turning these spaces from reliable carbon sinks to net carbon emitters – measures to protect these areas from fire risk, in addition to other forms of disruption, are therefore essential to maintain their role in climate mitigation. Interestingly, research suggests that greater compositional and structural diversity within forests can reduce biomass loss from wildfires, as well as windthrows and insect outbreaks. This highlights the additional protective value of biodiversity-enhancing measures, which help maintain ecosystem resilience and, in turn, their climate regulation capacity. For instance, insect outbreaks can influence wildfire severity by increasing the quantity and distribution of combustible material. 

Ultimately, protecting biodiversity, habitats and natural resources preserves the ability of ecosystems to sequester and store carbon – restoring them not only support species survival, but it can also improve the net absorption, storage, and climate mitigation potential of natural environments.
