Summary – Pathway 3.1: Sustainable farming strategies
3.1.1 Adaptation measures
Sustainable farming strategies encompass a range of agricultural practices that aim to minimise the impact of farming activities on the environment and to meet current food needs without compromising future generations’ ability to do the same. While not exhaustive, the following is a list of major strategies that can be subsumed under sustainable farming:
	•	Conservation Agriculture is a farming system that promotes minimum soil disturbance (i.e. no tillage), maintenance of a permanent soil cover, and diversification of plant species. It enhances biodiversity and natural biological processes above and below the ground surface, which contribute to increased water and nutrient use efficiency and to improved and sustained crop production. 
	•	Minimum soil disturbance refers to reduced tillage practices like direct seeding or fertilizer placement, which minimize disruption to the soil. This approach improves soil health, reduces erosion and fuel use, enhances biodiversity, and supports climate change adaptation and mitigation through better water regulation, carbon storage, and increased soil fertility.
	•	Soil organic cover involves using crop residues or cover crops to protect and enrich the soil. It helps prevent erosion, retain moisture, suppress weeds, recycle nutrients, improve soil structure, and boost biodiversity, all while reducing the need for chemical inputs and enhancing resilience in farming systems.
	•	Crop diversification involves growing multiple crop species through rotation or association, enhancing soil health, water use, and pest control. It boosts yield stability, strengthens resilience to climate change, and reduces greenhouse gas emissions.
	•	Organic farming is a sustainable agricultural approach focused on producing food using natural substances and processes, avoiding synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and GMOs. Its key aims include enhancing soil fertility, maintaining water quality, preserving biodiversity, and promoting ecological balance. It also ensures high standards of animal welfare by requiring practices that meet animals' natural behavioural needs. 
	•	The use of climate-adapted crops can reduce the impacts of drought and water scarcity by improving resilience and productivity under changing conditions. While implementation costs may vary, these crops can enhance soil carbon storage, support biodiversity, and influence ecosystem services such as pollination, nutrient cycling, and carbon sequestration, depending on associated farming practices.
	•	Agroecology is an integrated approach to farming that combines ecological science with social values to design sustainable and resilient food systems. It focuses on improving interactions between plants, animals, people, and the environment by promoting practices like crop diversification, organic soil management, and community-based food systems. As both a scientific discipline and a grassroots movement, agroecology supports local solutions, respects cultural and environmental contexts, and aims to achieve multiple goals—such as food security, climate resilience, biodiversity conservation, and social equity—through holistic, locally adapted strategies.
	•	Agroforestry combines trees with agriculture on the same land, integrating crops and/or animals to enhance ecological and economic benefits. It includes silvopastoral and silvoarable agroforestry, forest farming, hedgerows and riparian buffer strips, and home or kitchen gardens. These practices aim at improving resource use efficiency, boosting biodiversity, aiding climate change mitigation, and providing economic resilience by diversifying income. 
	•	Precision agriculture is a farming approach that uses advanced technologies – such as sensors, GPS, drones, and data analysis – to monitor and manage crops with high accuracy. It aims to optimize resource use and improve productivity by tailoring actions to specific conditions within fields.
	•	Modifying crop calendars can help farmers better align planting and harvesting with changing climate conditions, improving moisture use and reducing drought risks. This low-cost adaptation can increase yields, enhance soil carbon storage, and affect both above-ground and soil biodiversity, supporting overall ecosystem services.
	•	Integrated Pest Management (IPM) requires the management of pests using a combination of biological, physical, and other non-chemical methods, resorting to pesticides only when necessary. IPM focuses on preventing and suppressing harmful organisms through practices like crop rotation, resistant crop varieties, balanced fertilization, hygiene measures, and preserving beneficial species. Its goal is to minimize risks to human health and the environment, aiming to maintain healthy crops with minimal ecosystem disruption.
	•	Rotational grazing involves moving livestock between different pastures to allow rest and regrowth of forage. This system improves pasture productivity, quality, and digestibility, and reduces overgrazing and selective feeding. It is especially effective in humid climates and can lead to increased forage yields, particularly in ruminants.
	•	Regenerative agriculture is a broad, evolving approach to sustainable farming that focuses on restoring soil health and reversing biodiversity loss, while also maintaining productivity and enhancing ecosystem services like carbon storage. Unlike fixed-rule systems (e.g., organic farming), regenerative agriculture is goal-oriented and adaptable, allowing selective use of modern tools such as tillage, fertilizers, and breeding technologies when they contribute to environmental improvement. It aims to go beyond reducing harm by creating positive environmental outcomes, such as improved carbon sequestration and ecosystem restoration. 
	•	Carbon farming describes agricultural practices that reduce greenhouse gas emissions, enhance carbon sequestration, and avoid carbon loss in soils and vegetation. These activities can include cover cropping, reduced or no tillage, agroforestry, rotational grazing, crop rotation and diversification, and organic soil amendments. 
3.1.2 Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change
As each sustainable farming strategy targets different facets of agricultural impacts, their intended outcomes as a climate adaptation tool vary and are context specific. However, the main objectives from adopting sustainable farming strategies can be summarised as follows:
	•	Reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from agricultural practices
	•	Capturing and storing carbon through sequestration in soil and vegetation and maintaining existing carbon stores
	•	Protecting and improving water quality and enhancing water use efficiency
	•	Increasing climate resilience and adaptation to climate extremes and shocks
	•	Delivering ecosystem services such as nutrient cycling, erosion control, pollination, and pest regulation
	•	Boosting soil health and preventing soil degradation
	•	Protecting and enhancing biodiversity
	•	Reduce reliance on chemical inputs, such as synthetic fertilizers and pesticides, as well as antibiotics for animals.
	•	Promoting economic sustainability by diversifying incomes or lowering production costs
	•	Supporting animal welfare
3.1.3 Co-benefits for infectious disease
3.1.3.1 Providing safe and clean water resources
Many sustainable farming strategies aim to reduce or eliminate the use of chemical inputs, such as synthetic fertilisers, pesticides, and antibiotics, into agricultural production. Besides representing a direct risk for human health via exposure, these chemical inputs constitute a key source of environmentally harmful contamination of ground water and nearby or downstream water bodies. Sustainable farming strategies that tackle their proliferation thus reduce subsequent water contamination and contribute to safer and cleaner water resources. Key approaches to achieve this are IPM and organic farming which both seek to substitute chemical inputs with non-synthetic alternatives, as well as crop rotation and precision farming, which reduce the need for fertilisers and pesticides through diversification of planted species and resource use efficiency gains. While the provision of safe and clean water is an explicit target outcome of many agricultural climate adaptation and mitigation measures, it also offers potential co-benefits for human exposure to infectious diseases. 
Besides chemical inputs, another key source of agriculturally induced water contamination is nutrient runoff, particularly of excess nitrogen and phosphorus, into adjacent and downstream water bodies. Subsequent nutrient pollution – or eutrophication – poses a significant hazard to aquatic ecosystems, human and animal health, and the provision of drinking water. A key strategy to prevent nutrient spillage into waterways is to enhance soil health and reduce erosion, thereby increasing the retention of nutrients in the soil. This is an intended outcome of conservation farming approaches such as minimum tillage and soil organic cover, but also carries co-benefits of providing safe and clean water that impact positively on human exposure to infectious disease. Similar co-benefits can be expected for farming strategies that reduce the injection of nutrients into the soil, such as rotational grazing, crop rotation, precision agriculture, IPM, and organic farming. 
Examples
	•	Improving soil structure of an arable crop farm in the district of Heilbronn (Germany): As part of the AgriAdapt project, a 240ha farm in southwestern Germany implemented soil structure improvement measures to adapt to rising temperatures, drier conditions, and more frequent climate extremes. Among the intended outcomes are avoiding nutrient loss and reducing erosion, which is achieved through crop diversification, cover crops, reduced tillage and no tillage on hilly farmland, the use of climate-adapted crop species, and modified sowing calendars. This also reduces the need for synthetic or natural fertilisers and pesticides. The adopted strategies thus produce the co-benefit of improving the quality of nearby water resources by minimising chemical and nutrient contamination through soil erosion and runoff.
	•	Crop diversification and improved soil management for adaptation to climate change in Segovia (Spain): As part of the AgriAdapt project, a 110ha organic farm in central Spain implemented crop and soil adaptation measures to improve resilience against increasingly extreme climatic conditions in the region. Strategies used include low tillage, more frequent organic matter applications, improved crop rotations, crop diversification, use of more climatic resistant crop variants, and creation of multifunction field margins. The latter in particular have contributed to lower soil erosion, thus reducing nutrient runoff and water contamination.
3.1.3.2 Healthy ecosystems
A key adaptation goal of many sustainable farming strategies is to reduce the prevalence and vulnerabilities of monocultural agriculture and instead create farmlands that more closely mimic natural ecosystems. This tends to enhance biodiversity and climate resilience while also allowing the land to be both agriculturally productive and fulfil important ecosystem functions, e.g. pollination. It also offers significant co-benefits by creating healthier ecosystems which can reduce the hazard posed by infectious diseases. Agroforestry, for example, integrates crops and/or livestock with perennial trees and shrubs, creating more biodiverse and ecological environments. Healthy ecosystems at a small scale can also be created by incorporating riparian strips around the edges of fields or pastures. Cover crops provide an organic soil cover that functions as a ground-level micro-habitat for many organisms. Biodiversity and ecosystem health can also be enhanced by adopting organic farming strategies, avoiding the use of pesticides that harm non-target organisms. Equally, regenerative agriculture and crop diversification offer co-benefits of reducing monocultural farming and contributing to healthier ecosystems. 
Examples
	•	Agroforestry: agriculture of the future? The case of Montpellier: The SAFE project implemented a walnut-wheat agroforestry system to address rising temperatures, drought, and soil degradation. This approach increased productivity by 40% compared to separate cultivation and created important co-benefits for ecosystem health by enhancing biodiversity, improving soil structure and water retention, and reducing erosion. The integration of trees and crops supports beneficial organisms and promotes ecological balance, while also providing farmers with diversified income sources. 
	•	Autonomous adaptation to droughts in an agro-silvo-pastoral system in Alentejo, Portugal: Herdade do Freixo do Meio, a multifunctional farm, combines agroforestry, livestock, and regenerative practices to adapt to increasing droughts. The integration of drought-tolerant trees and diverse crops improves soil structure and water retention, while organic management enhances soil microbial activity. Water harvesting and efficient irrigation reduce water stress, supporting biodiversity and ecosystem resilience. These measures create healthy ecosystems that sustain productivity and climate adaptability, serving as a model for sustainable farming in Mediterranean climates.
3.1.3.3 Reduced risk of AMR
There exist complex interlinkages between climate change and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). For one, climate change can exacerbate the emergence of AMR. Higher temperatures are associated with increased bacterial growth, while an increase in climate variability has been shown to enhance microbial adaptation. Both of these can facilitate the emergence and the horizontal transfer of resistant genes, thus contributing to AMR. With rising temperatures, these resistant pathogens are then able to spread further than before. In addition, it has been shown how warmer temperatures can lead to higher concentrations of heavy metals and biocides in soil and water environments and higher uptake of these elements by bacteria. This risks the development of AMR through co-resistance mechanisms.  Intensive livestock agriculture is among the leading co-drivers of both climate change and AMR through GHG emissions and the proliferation of antimicrobials. Furthermore, human interference in terrestrial ecosystems, in particular deforestation and changes in land use, such as conversion of areas into farmland, facilitates AMR emergence through changes in the soil microbiome. Consequently, the adoption of sustainable farming strategies that mitigate or adapt to the effects of climate change can potentially offer significant co-benefits for the fight against AMR, and thus reduce human vulnerability to infectious diseases. Agroforestry, for example, minimises the disruption to the soil microbiome by retaining trees and/or shrubs as part of a healthier agricultural ecosystem. Organic farming approaches minimise or eliminate the use of chemical inputs, including antibiotic drugs, thus reducing the risks of emerging bacterial resistance. Similarly, sustainable agricultural practices that improve animal or plant health, such as crop rotation, IPM, or rotational grazing, may reduce the need for antibiotics and biocides. IPM provides additional co-benefits to combatting AMR as it tends to combine various forms of pest control mechanisms, thus delaying the evolution of resistance compared to single treatment approaches.
Examples
	•	Reducing antibiotic use in livestock through agroecology in France: France's national ÉcoAntibio plans (2012–2016, 2017–2022, 2023-2028) aim to reduce antibiotic use in livestock by promoting agroecological practices. The initiative straddles the intersections of agriculture, climate adaptation, and health policy. Run by the Ministry of Agriculture and Food as part of its agroecology programme, the plans explicitly align themself with both the Ministry of Health’s AMR strategy and the Ministry of Environment’s national environmental health plan. Between 2011 and 2022, animal exposure to antibiotics in France reduced by 52 per cent, with exposure to those antibiotics with critical importance for human health reduced by over 90 per cent, thus contributing to the fight against AMR.
	•	Responsible use of Burkholderia bacteria for climate adaptation: The case of Burkholderia bacteria is indicative for the complex interactions between climate adaptation and AMR. The bacterial genus can promote plant growth through fixing nitrogen, solubilising phosphorus, and producing growth hormones, while also degrading pollutants, such as pesticides. It offers particular potential in inoculating crops in climate-affected areas with soils experiencing increased aridity or acidification. However, while some strands are beneficial and harmless to people, others can act as a potential pathogen for humans, being linked to pneumonia and septicaemia, and exhibit resistance against multiple antimicrobials. Therefore, responsible and targeted use, as well as continued research and monitoring, is necessary to harness the climate adaptation potential of Burkholderia while preventing any trade-offs for infectious disease.

3.1.3.4 Improved food safety 
Climate-smart and sustainable farming practices can directly enhance food safety and reduce the risk of infectious foodborne diseases, first, by creating healthier ecosystems that improve biodiversity and, in doing so, reduce opportunities for infectious disease emergence, and second, by reducing contamination risks (as detailed in both co-benefit 3.1.3.1 and 3.1.3.2). Organic, regenerative, and other ‘low input’ systems, in minimising synthetic chemical use, help to prevent the accumulation of pesticide residues and heavy metals in the environment, and, importantly, in the food chain. Reducing the use (and misuse) of antibiotics in agriculture is also central to limiting antimicrobial resistance (AMR), helping to safeguard the microbiological safety of food systems and reducing the risk that foodborne pathogens become more challenging to address (as explored in co-benefit 3.1.3.3).   Integrated Pest Management, for example, reduces the risk of foodborne diseases, combining biological, cultural, and mechanical control methods to maintain pest populations below harmful thresholds while minimising reliance on synthetic pesticides. This strategy limits chemical runoff and microbial imbalances that can foster foodborne pathogens, thereby enhancing both environmental and consumer safety. Healthy soils maintained through composting, crop rotation, and microbial diversity further help to suppress harmful microorganisms and improve nutrient cycling, creating natural defences against potential contamination and infection. 
3.1.4 Trade-offs for infectious disease
3.1.4.1 Increased habitats for disease pathogens & vectors
Many sustainable farming strategies, such as agroforestry, cover crops, or organic agriculture, seek to enhance biodiversity and more closely mimic natural ecosystems, either by reducing the use of harmful pesticides or by creating natural habitats for wild animals and plants within utilised agricultural areas. While producing positive climate adaptation and mitigation outcomes, as well as potential co-benefits for reducing infectious disease risks (see 3.1.3.2), these strategies may inadvertently increase potential habitats for disease pathogens and vectors. (Agro-)forested and ground layer ecosystems often form ideal habitats for ticks and other invertebrate vectors, while riparian buffers adjacent to waterways can support mosquito breeding grounds. Larger invertebrate diversity and populations also tend to attract other potential carriers of zoonotic diseases, such as bats or rodents, the latter of which can serve either as direct vectors or as hosts for lice or fleas.
These increased habitats for pathogens and vectors are of particular concern as they are fully integrated within agriculturally active land with frequent interactions between humans and potential vectors, and therefore carry significant risk of zoonotic transmission of pathogens.
