Trade-off of social unrest, displacement of local communities, mental health risks   5.1.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change.   Seasonality affects most destinations in the EU, albeit to different degrees. In countries like Spain and Greece, the burden on tourism resources and destinations is highly concentrated in summer months. The EU regions with the highest levels of seasonality are largely coastal areas, whereas “destination” capital cities in temperate zones, such as Rome and Paris, experience tourism more evenly year-round. August remains the top tourism month in most EU regions, coinciding with the holiday period for many Europeans.  However, climate change presents significant challenges for the tourism sector: rising temperatures, sea-level rise, and the increasing frequency of droughts and floods threaten the viability of traditional travel patterns and tourism seasons. Tourism, while sometimes viewed by locals as a burden, is a vital economic force for many European countries, and the effects of climate change could lead to the restructuring of travel patterns and diminished viability of certain destinations. For example, declining snow cover in mountainous regions could jeopardise winter tourism, while sea level rise places coastal tourism at risk. Extreme heat throughout Europe can equally provoke significant losses for warmer tourist destinations.   Beyond the impacts of climate change, seasonal tourism itself can have negative consequences for a region. Concentration of tourism in a limited number of months, such as July and August, can have a negative impact on the environment, as it handles increased waste production and an increase in energy and water use. It can negatively impact quality of services, such as transport and health, to the detriment of local communities. Hyper-seasonality can also undermine economic performance, as tourism-specific infrastructure and labour are only used for part of the year, creating higher operational costs and lower profitability.   Consequently, the ‘de-seasonalisation’ or expansion of tourism seasons is a critical adaptation strategy to address the risks posed by climate change. Extending tourism beyond the traditional summer months helps reduce the sector’s vulnerability to extreme weather events and avoids economic losses tied to periods of unbearable heat, drought, or other disruptions. Shifting tourist seasons towards spring and autumn can offer more comfortable weather conditions for travellers, while reducing pressure on specific destinations and ecosystems during peak summer months.   Deseasonalisation can equally lower environmental stress on ecosystems and resources. Water resources, for example, which are often overexploited in summer, can be conserved and more evenly managed throughout the year, an essential practice in the face of rising water scarcity and water conflicts in Europe. Reducing seasonality can help avoid resource shortages and the potential associated conflict, such as that observed in Sicily, where water use for tourist infrastructure is starkly contrasted with the drought facing farmers and the local agricultural sector. Expanding the tourism season can thus help protect ecosystems from overuse, without compromising annual economic returns.   Expanding European tourism seasons is therefore a critical adaptation strategy to prevent economic and ecosystem losses associated with climate change and overtourism; a more even and equally distributed flow of tourism can serve to enhance service quality, support long-term employment and investment, and increase the resilience of the sector to climate change.    5.1.2. Adaptation measures  - Publicity and advertising campaigns:
This can be relevant for both internal and external markets. Domestic tourism is equally expected to increase as a result of climate change.  - Pricing to stimulate tourism expansion and competitiveness outside the high season - Promotion of seasonal activities outside traditional tourist peaks, e.g., Christmas markets, festivals, events, rather than beach or sun-related activities    - Promotion programs - Diversification of tourist activities, e.g. ski tourism to mountain tourism (see also 5.2. Expand tourism in new locations)  - Decrease average length of stay? - Promotion of alternative tourism models that are less weather dependent, e.g., gastronomic, indoor cultural experiences, sports etc.  - Promotion of offers that meet needs of off-season tourists, e.g., wellness experiences, spas which are popular in colder months  - Voluntary tourism exchange mechanism between Member States: This refers to the CALYPSO initiative adopted by the European Parliament, the main aim of which is out-of-season exchanges of tourists in four target groups (young and elderly people, people with reduced mobility and low-income families), particularly during the low season. - Online platforms designed to facilitate out-of-season tourism: The Social Tourism European Exchanges Platform (STEEP) aims to facilitate off-season transnational travel and tourism, in order to expand tourism seasons, promote less-known destinations and encourage longer-lasting employment in the tourism industry.   5.1.3. Co-benefit of improved economic stability and reduced short-term migration  Extending the tourist season and redistributing visitor flows can generate more consistent income for businesses in tourist-dependent areas. This helps reduce the need for seasonal layoffs and the reliance on short-term labour migration, which can improve economic stability for local communities. In turn, this greater stability and reduced short-term migration can have positive implications for infectious disease risk.  Economic stability plays a critical role in reducing human vulnerability to infectious diseases. While all populations are potentially at risk, it is well established that social, economic, and political inequalities can exacerbate this vulnerability. By increasing economic stability, those from poorer socioeconomic demographics are potentially more protected and less vulnerable to disease. Strengthening economic stability, especially for individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, can therefore help decrease susceptibility to infectious diseases and vulnerability to disease-related hardship.  More broadly, consistent economic conditions help sustain public health systems. By ensuring continuous funding and support for health infrastructure, communities are better equipped to respond to outbreaks and maintain preparedness. Simply put, a stronger and more stable health system improves disease resilience.   An expanded tourism season can also support longer-term employment contracts, reducing the need for short-term migration or sudden inflows of workers during peak periods. Movements of people, whether for leisure or work, contributes to an increase in the risk of disease transmission; just like tourists, seasonal or short-term workers may bring infectious diseases with them or return home with them, thus increasing the risk of exposure across multiple regions. Short-term migration has been linked to the circulation of various pathogens, including those responsible for waterborne diseases, such as cholera, and vector-borne diseases like dengue. Reducing the need for such migration through a more balanced, year-round tourism model can therefore have the added co-benefit of lowering disease risk by limiting the movement of people between regions and the accompanying potential of exposure to infectious disease.  
  5.1.4. Co-benefit of reduced overcrowding   Expanding the tourist season can help distribute tourist flows more evenly throughout the year, reducing overcrowding during peak periods. This redistribution can, in turn, lower the risk of infectious disease transmission associated with overcrowding.   Overcrowded environments heighten the likelihood of disease spread, as individuals are more frequently in close proximity, speaking, eating, coughing, sneezing, and interacting in ways that facilitate transmission. As highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic, densely populated settings such as restaurants and tourist hotspots can significantly increase the incidence of infectious diseases. Greater physical spacing between individuals reduces opportunities for transmission.  By expanding tourism seasons in a way that helps mitigate peak-time overcrowding – rather than simply increasing the total number of tourists – regions can therefore help lower the risk of infectious disease transmission.   5.1.5. Co-benefit of reduced pressure on health services  Expanding the tourism season to achieve a more balanced distribution of visitor flows can ease the pressure placed on local health services during peak periods. Managing tourism more evenly throughout the year allows health systems to operate under more stable and predictable conditions, which can support more effective public health planning and resource allocation. This in turn, helps strengthen the resilience of health services to disease outbreaks, reducing the vulnerability of both residents and visitors to disease transmission.  As climate change patterns are expected to increase the pressures on public health systems, (through incidences such as extreme weather events, water scarcity, and ecosystem disruption), ensuring that healthcare infrastructure is not further burdened by seasonal tourism peaks becomes increasingly important. Redistributing tourism flows through expanded seasons therefore offers additional benefits for infectious disease risk and the resilience of health services to infectious diseases. 

5.1.6. Co-benefit of contributing to healthy ecosystems  Seasonal tourism can place significant environmental pressure on a region. The concentration of visitors during peak months, typically July and August, leads to surges in waste production, energy consumption, and water use, placing a serious strain on ecosystems and depleting local resources. As a result, expanding and redistributing tourism across the year can contribute meaningfully to ecosystem health. Water resources, for example, which are often overexploited in summer, can be better conserved and more evenly managed throughout year-round, an essential practice in the face of rising water scarcity and water conflicts in Europe.  When measures to expand the tourist season alleviate environmental pressures and support ecosystem health, they equally create benefits for resilience to infectious diseases. This stems from the fact that biodiversity and ecological balance are central to maintaining ecosystem resilience: in degraded ecosystems and areas of weakened genetic and species diversity, the complexity and heterogeneity of the natural system are compromised, which makes it more vulnerable and at a heightened risk of creating new opportunities for disease emergence. Notably, the risk of infectious zoonotic diseases is often linked to the degradation or destruction of ecosystems.   Consequently, measures that expand and redistribute tourism flows more evenly throughout the year can reduce environmental stress on vulnerable ecosystems, improve overall ecosystem health, and help limit the presence and spread of disease vectors, thereby reducing infectious disease risk.   5.1.7. Trade-off of shift in disease patterns  Expanding the tourist season may carry the unintended consequence of prolonging the period during which both visitors and local populations are exposed to infectious diseases. Increased mobility over a longer timeframe is likely to elevate the frequency and reach of infectious disease outbreaks, as continuous human movement facilitates greater opportunities for transmission. A longer tourism season can therefore extend the window of exposure, increasing the risk of infectious disease.    5.1.8. Trade-off of difficulty in detecting diseases in returning travellers   Expanding tourism seasons may increase the accessibility and appeal of certain destinations, leading to more returning travellers. As their numbers rise, so too does the incidence of imported cases of diseases such as malaria, dengue fever, diarrhoeal illnesses, rickettsial and other tick-borne infections, and respiratory diseases. This adds to an already significant challenge: timely detection and diagnosis of disease.   Several factors can complicate detection: healthcare providers in non-endemic regions may be unfamiliar with the clinical presentation of certain illnesses, delaying accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment; overlapping symptoms between climate-sensitive diseases and common infections, such as flu, can add further difficulty; long incubation periods mean symptoms may not appear until long after return; and local healthcare providers may lack specialised tests for ‘tropical’ diseases which are most prevalent in Africa, Asia, and the Caribbean. Yet, as climate-sensitive diseases emerge as risks in specific European regions, travellers are more likely to return with conditions healthcare providers are less accustomed to treating, and unlikely to associate with European travel. For instance, while governmental advice commonly stresses vaccinations and preparation for tropical diseases before travel to Asia or Africa, it does not yet reflect the growing risk of dengue or malaria in Southern Europe. Research also highlights that a major challenge in preventing tick-borne encephalitis, for example, is travellers’ very low awareness of the risk when visiting industrialised European countries.  These diagnostic difficulties increase the risk of prolonged exposure and transmission. People may underestimate their symptoms, delay seeking care, or unknowingly spread disease within their communities.Consequently, the expansion of tourist seasons may carry the trade-off of inadvertently raising the number of returning travellers carrying difficult-to-diagnose infections, heightening public health risks associated with disease exposure. 

5.1.9. Trade-off of social unrest, displacement of local communities, mental health risks   Expanding the tourist season can increase visitor numbers and drive investments in infrastructure and services designed to accommodate them. However, such developments can risk displacing local communities, weakening small businesses, and disrupting traditional socioeconomic structures, livelihoods, and cultural knowledge systems - whether policies that increase that the availability of short-term rentals, strain local property markets, or fuel sustained high prices (rather than those concentrated in peak seasons), expanding tourist infrastructure can threaten community stability.   Additionally, unlike seasonal peaks, constant pressure on public resources such as water or transport, gives local residents less opportunity to adapt or prepare. The redirection of funding towards tourism infrastructure may also come at the expense of local services, for example, by prioritising improvements to transport networks along tourist routes rather than in peripheral or residential areas.   Over time, increasing tourism, gentrification, and displacement is likely to provoke social unrest and mental health concerns among affected populations, both of which carry consequences for infectious disease risk. Across Europe, rising tourist numbers are linked to growing public resistance, marked by anti-tourism protests and sentiments of tourism-phobia. Displacement and unrest can increase exposure to infectious diseases, whether it manifests through relocation to concentrated housing developments, increased local population mobility, or public demonstrations. These conditions can increase the risk of infectious disease transmission, as population density facilitates the spread of gastrointestinal and respiratory infections.   Moreover, displacement and social unrest can trigger or exacerbate stress, anxiety, depression and other mental health conditions. These impacts are not only harmful in themselves but also reduce human resilience to disease, as poor mental health can impair immune function, reducing the body’s ability to combat pathogens and potentially leading to more severe disease outcomes. In this way, tourism expansion, when linked with displacement and social unrest, may inadvertently heighten human vulnerability to infectious diseases due to the mental health burden these can impose.
