Expand tourism in new locations  5.2.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change  Climate change presents significant challenges for the tourism sector: rising temperatures, sea-level rise, and the increasing frequency of droughts and floods threaten the viability and appeal of certain tourism destinations. Tourism, while sometimes viewed by locals as a burden, is a vital economic force for many European countries, and the effects of climate change could lead to revenue losses due to shifting travel patterns from the restructuring of travel models and the reduced viability of specific destinations. For example, declining snow cover in mountainous regions could jeopardise winter tourism, while sea level rise places coastal tourism at risk. Extreme heat throughout Europe can equally provoke significant losses for warmer tourist destinations.   Consequently, the expansion of tourism into new and less commonly visited locations is a critical adaptation strategy to address the risks posed by climate change. Expanding tourism beyond typical destinations helps reduce the sector’s vulnerability to extreme weather events and avoid economic losses tied to the increasing likelihood of unbearable heat, drought, or other disruptions. Expanding tourism in new locations with more temperate, predictable or climate-resilient environments can offer consistently comfortable weather for travellers, thereby helping to sustain steady tourism levels while drawing visitors to previously underexplored regions.  Expanding tourism into new, less frequented areas can also diversify tourism destinations, lowering environmental stress on the ecosystems and resources of traditionally popular, or heavily burdened locations. Water resources, for example, which risk being overexploited during summer in warm tourist destinations, can be conserved and more evenly managed throughout the year, an essential practice in the face of rising water scarcity and water conflicts in Europe. Diversifying tourism locations can help avoid resource shortages and the potential associated conflict, such as that observed in Sicily, where water use for tourist infrastructure is starkly contrasted with the drought facing farmers and the local agricultural sector. Expanding tourism in new locations can thus help distribute the pressures of tourism and protect ecosystems from overexploitation, without compromising annual economic returns.   Expanding tourism in new places is therefore a critical adaptation strategy to prevent economic and ecosystem losses associated with climate change and overtourism; a more even and equally distributed flow of tourism can help maintain service quality, support stable employment and investment in the industry, and bring economic opportunities to emerging destinations, strengthening the sector’s overall resilience to climate change.     5.2.2. Adaptation measures  - Publicity and advertising campaigns for new and rarely explored destinations: This can be relevant for both internal and external markets. Domestic tourism is equally expected to increase as a result of climate change.  - Promotion programs for new and rarely explored destinations - Diversification of tourism activities, e.g. cooler summertime mountain tourism rather than a focus on warm coastal tourism  - Promotion of new locations that offer experiences that are not particularly weather dependent, e.g., gastronomic, indoor cultural experiences, sports etc. - Building and revitalising trails, nature parks, eco-retreats, etc. in new locations  - Hosting events in new places, e.g. festivals  - Creating infrastructure to make new tourist destinations more accessible, e.g., Norwegian cruises, increased flight schedules, subsidised hotel development, new bus lines (see also 5.3. Improve infrastructures & resources for tourists). 


5.2.3. Co-benefit of reduced overcrowding   Measures to expand tourism into new locations can help distribute visitor numbers more evenly across a region, easing overcrowding in traditionally popular destinations. This redistribution can, in turn, lower the risk of infectious disease transmission associated with overcrowding.   Overcrowded environments heighten the likelihood of disease spread, as individuals are more frequently in close proximity, speaking, eating, coughing, sneezing, and interacting in ways that facilitate transmission. As highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic, densely populated settings such as restaurants and tourist hotspots can significantly increase the incidence of infectious diseases. Greater physical spacing between individuals reduces opportunities for transmission.  By expanding tourism into new locations in ways that redistribute visitors and alleviate overcrowding in popular destinations – rather than simply increasing the total number of tourists – regions can therefore help lower the risk of infectious disease transmission.   5.2.4. Co-benefit of contributing to healthy ecosystems  If expanding tourism in new locations is directed towards the clean-up, restoration or rewilding of degraded areas, these investments may also yield important co-benefits for resilience to infectious diseases. Restoration, rewilding and nature-based solutions increasingly form part of broader trends in ‘sustainable’ and ‘regenerative’ tourism, promoting environmental stewardship alongside economic development.   Where tourism-oriented investments in new locations follow such an approach, they have the potential to restore and improve the overall health of local ecosystems, which in turn carries important benefits for infectious disease resilience. This stems from the fact that biodiversity and ecological balance are central to maintaining ecosystem resilience: in degraded ecosystems and areas of weakened genetic and species diversity, the complexity and heterogeneity of the natural system are compromised, which makes it more vulnerable and at a heightened risk of creating new opportunities for disease emergence. Notably, the risk of infectious zoonotic diseases is often linked to the degradation or destruction of ecosystems.   Consequently, investing in the restoration of natural areas that were previously degraded, in ways that strengthen ecosystem health, can help limit the presence and spread of disease vectors, thereby reducing the risk of infectious disease.    This benefit is especially pronounced when investments in new locations target previously neglected areas that would otherwise be at risk of environmentally destructive economic uses, such as mining, intensive agriculture, or industrial development.   However, the benefits for infectious disease resilience associated with expanding tourism into new locations are strictly dependent on the success of efforts to improve degraded ecosystem health, rather than on the development or increased use of pristine, intact, or already healthy natural areas. 

5.2.5. Trade-off of ecological disruption
 Expanding tourism into new locations, particularly those situated in or adjacent to natural environments, can carry trade-offs for infectious disease resilience, as it may disrupt ecosystems that have not historically been exposed to significant human activity. The adverse impacts of tourists on local environments stem not only from their physical presence, but equally from the ecological pressures their presence introduces: direct environmental impacts include trampling, wildlife disturbance, and littering, while broader indirect or cumulative effects include increased air and water pollution, reduced freshwater availability, greater wastewater output, loss of forest cover, and accelerated soil erosion. Inexperienced, or non-local tourists, unfamiliar with the sensitivities of particular ecosystems, can also heighten the risk of catastrophic sudden events, such as human-induced wildfires.   When new tourist flows exert pressure on natural resources, promote development in previously undisturbed areas, and increase pollution, waste, and human-wildlife interactions, they place ecosystems at risk of disruption and degradation - an ecological disruption which has direct implications for disease resilience. Biodiverse and healthy ecosystems provide a range of habitats and promote genetic variability, which can create natural checks on the spread of pathogens; variability in natural systems buffers against disease transmission by preventing the dominance of single disease vectors or hosts. When biodiversity is lost, this resilience weakens. In fact, ecosystem degradation and biodiversity loss have been identified as the primary environmental driver of infectious diseases.  Consequently, measures to introduce and expand tourism into new locations risk disrupting and compromising ecosystem health, which may inadvertently increase vulnerability to disease outbreaks.  
  5.2.6. Trade-off of shift in disease patterns   Expanding tourism in new locations can carry the unintended consequence of increasing exposure to diseases among both visitors and local populations. Increased mobility of travellers is likely to elevate the frequency and expand the reach of infectious disease outbreaks, as continuous human movement facilitates greater opportunities for transmission. As tourism reaches previously less-visited regions, it can extend exposure to a broader range of areas and communities, thereby elevating overall infectious disease risk.  This expansion may also introduce diseases unfamiliar to resident populations, which may lack prior exposure or immunity, further increasing local vulnerability to infectious disease. These new patterns of movement thus risk reshaping disease patterns in ways that can increase exposure and transmission across regions.   
5.2.7. Trade-off of limited capacity of healthcare systems  Expanding tourism into new destinations, without adequate financial investment and strengthening of healthcare infrastructure, inherently risks compromising infectious disease resilience. Local healthcare systems in new tourist locations are likely to be unaccustomed to managing increased volumes of (potentially foreign) patients, and may lack the budget, materials and linguistic tools to effectively treat them.  An increase in the number of travellers not only presents the challenge for local healthcare systems of a surge in demand, but equally carries the risk of introducing diseases unfamiliar to the resident population, which may lack prior exposure or immunity.    Furthermore, tourism’s seasonal nature often demands short-term labour, a staffing practice that healthcare systems are unlikely to rely on. Without sustained funding to expand healthcare capacity year-round, local medical services in emerging tourist destinations risk becoming overwhelmed by travellers during peak seasons, potentially diminishing the overall quality and accessibility of care.     Robust, well-funded and prepared healthcare systems are critical for maintaining disease resilience; consequently, sudden influxes of tourists that place a disproportionate strain on local healthcare facilities risk heightening both travellers’ and residents’ overall vulnerability to infectious diseases. The expansion of tourism into new locations, without parallel healthcare reinforcement, therefore presents a significant trade-off for infectious disease risk, by undermining public health security in previously less visited destinations.   

5.2.8. Trade-off of difficulty in detecting exotic diseases in returning travellers  Expanding tourism in new locations may increase the accessibility and appeal of typically less-frequented destinations, leading to more returning travellers from these areas. As their numbers rise, so too does the incidence of imported cases of diseases such as malaria, dengue fever, diarrhoeal illnesses, rickettsial and other tick-borne infections, and respiratory diseases. This adds to an already significant challenge: timely detection and diagnosis of disease.   Several factors can complicate detection: healthcare providers in non-endemic regions may be unfamiliar with the clinical presentation of certain illnesses, delaying accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment; overlapping symptoms between climate-sensitive diseases and common infections, such as flu, can add further difficulty; long incubation periods mean symptoms may not appear until long after return; and local healthcare providers may lack specialised tests for ‘tropical’ diseases which are most prevalent in Africa, Asia, and the Caribbean. Yet, as climate-sensitive diseases emerge as risks in specific European regions, travellers are more likely to return with conditions healthcare providers are less accustomed to treating, and unlikely to associate with European travel. For instance, while governmental advice commonly stresses vaccinations and preparation for tropical diseases before travel to Asia or Africa, it does not yet reflect the growing risk of dengue or malaria in Southern Europe. Research also highlights that a major challenge in preventing tick-borne encephalitis, for example, is travellers’ very low awareness of the risk when visiting industrialised European countries.  These diagnostic difficulties increase the risk of prolonged exposure and transmission. People may underestimate their symptoms, delay seeking care, or unknowingly spread disease within their communities.Consequently, the expansion of tourist seasons may carry the trade-off of inadvertently raising the number of returning travellers carrying difficult-to-diagnose infections, heightening public health risks associated with disease exposure. 

5.2.9. Trade-off of social unrest, displacement of local communities, mental health risks

Expanding tourism in new locations can increase visitor numbers and stimulate the development of infrastructure, services, and resources designed to accommodate them. However, such developments can risk displacing local communities, weakening small businesses, and disrupting traditional socioeconomic structures, livelihoods, and cultural knowledge systems - whether through policy-led development of previously natural areas, rising property values driven by tourist demand, or sustained increases in the cost and scarcity of essential resources like water, energy, transport, and healthcare, these transformations can threaten community stability.   The redirection of local funding toward new tourism infrastructure may also divert resources away from essential public services, for example, by prioritising improvements to transport and amenities along tourist routes rather than in long-established residential areas.  Over time, increasing tourism, gentrification, and displacement is likely to provoke social unrest and mental health concerns among affected populations, both of which carry consequences for infectious disease risk. Across Europe, rising tourist numbers are linked to growing public resistance, marked by anti-tourism protests and sentiments of tourism-phobia. Displacement and unrest can increase exposure to infectious diseases, whether it manifests through relocation to concentrated housing developments, increased local population mobility, or public demonstrations. These conditions can increase the risk of infectious disease transmission, as population density facilitates the spread of gastrointestinal and respiratory infections.   Moreover, displacement and social unrest can trigger or exacerbate stress, anxiety, depression and other mental health conditions. These impacts are not only harmful in themselves but also reduce human resilience to disease, as poor mental health can impair immune function, reducing the body’s ability to combat pathogens and potentially leading to more severe disease outcomes. In this way, tourism expansion in new locations, when linked with displacement and social unrest, may inadvertently heighten human vulnerability to infectious diseases due to the mental health burden these can impose.
