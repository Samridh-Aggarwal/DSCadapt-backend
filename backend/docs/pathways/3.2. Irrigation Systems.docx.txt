 3.2. Irrigation systems
 Irrigation is the process of delivering water to plants to support their needs for several important resources. While water is a primary component of irrigation, it also delivers dissolved oxygen and nutrients to the plant – both of which are essential for photosynthesis and continued growth.   Irrigation systems deliver water to plants to fuel their growth – all irrigation systems are comprised of tubing or piping to transport water from the source to individual plants or larger areas of crops. These systems can range from basic manual methods to highly advanced technologies incorporating automatic timers, soil and moisture sensors, and even AI-based decision-making tools. They may incorporate mixing or blending tanks to ensure even distribution of any dissolved nutrients, as well as filters to remove insoluble particles. Systems may also be constructed in a way that supplies individual plants or designed to water entire fields using broad coverage methods, such as overhead spraying.   As of 2016, the European Commission reported that 10.2 million hectares of agricultural land were irrigated, with the highest levels found in Southern European countries, including Italy, Spain, and Greece, where irrigation is considered an essential element in many types of agricultural production. Both the demand and the capacity for irrigation vary depending on annual rainfall patterns, which are increasingly variable, as well as on the type of crop, as water needs differ significantly between species. Farming practices also play a role: while some methods rely heavily on irrigation, others, such as natural or biodynamic approaches, seek to minimise artificial watering (see 3.1. for further details). Given the increasing scarcity of freshwater resources, irrigation systems that enhance the precision and efficiency of water delivery are essential for conserving this vital input.  3.2.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change

Climate change threatens water supply in Europe in multiple ways, ranging from changes in precipitation which alter seasonal and annual water availability, increased evaporation due to higher temperatures, and extreme weather events that lead to droughts and floods. Increased likelihood of floods also places our water supply at risk of contamination from sewer overflows.   Water availability is closely linked with our health, a wide range of economic activities, and (fresh)water dependent ecosystems. Warming climates, and the increasing frequency and severity of extreme weather events therefore pose significant risks to health outcomes and can cause extensive economic damage.   Consequently, the EU Strategy on Adaptation to Climate Change emphasises the importance of sustainable water use in agriculture – this objective is primarily supported through the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP), which includes climate-smart farming guidelines, obligatory measures, and funding opportunities for environmental protection and eco-schemes. Adapting the irrigation sector is a priority given the potential intensification of water scarcity and the rising demand for food in regions experiencing economic development.   Examples of measures can be found in the Swedish Agricultural Agency’s National Adaptation Plan, which endorses increasing the creation of wetlands and small water bodies to support irrigation systems. As part of its focus on sustainable water management, Greece’s National Adaptation Plan prioritises the rationalisation of irrigation, scheduling water use according to plant need, maintaining aging irrigation networks to reduce losses, and the adoption of more efficient systems. Germany’s national strategy also incorporates quality standards to protect the environment and human health from potential negative impacts of irrigation.   When implemented sustainably, irrigation systems can help to conserve water, curb the energy used for pumping and distribution, and reduce labour demands, yielding collective savings in cost, energy and water, outcomes that are increasingly important as climate change both drives water scarcity, and calls for more energy efficiency across economic sectors. 
 Specific advanced irrigation technologies that contribute to climate adaptation include subsurface drip irrigation, a low-pressure, high efficiency irrigation system that uses buried drip tubes or tape to meet crop water needs. It has been shown to conserve water and improve crop yields by eliminating surface water evaporation, and, in arid areas, can also reduce the potential for weed germination.  Even surface-level drip irrigation has been shown to produce water savings of over 27% compared to conventional cultivation in rice fields. However, the choice of irrigation system is conditioned by factors such as crop type, soil characteristics, local climate conditions, cost, and other site-specific considerations.  A notable example of high-tech sustainable irrigation is smart precision irrigation, a highly effective method for improving water efficiency in agriculture and landscaping.  Smart precision systems incorporate modern technologies, such as the Internet of Things (IoT), wireless sensor networks, and data analytics, to optimise irrigation processes by monitoring environmental parameters including weather conditions, soil moisture and temperature, in real time. This allows for precise, site-specific control of the amount of water used. 
 In contrast, an example of a low-tech sustainable approach is rainwater harvesting, which involves collecting and storing rainwater for irrigation purposes. This method may be particularly well-suited to small urban green spaces which can be manually irrigated. Rainwater is often an underutilized resource and can be captured from rooftops or other surfaces and reused efficiently, contributing to water conservation and even supporting stormwater management through prevented runoff. 
 Overall, sustainable irrigation systems are an effective and multifaceted adaptation measure, helping to conserve water resources, reduce energy consumption, lower labour requirements, and generate cost savings, (among other benefits). In doing so, they support continued agricultural productivity and contribute to making the European food system more resilient to the impacts of climate change.   3.2.2. Adaptation measures
 - Spray/Sprinkler irrigation: Examples include:
	•	Centre pivot irrigation, in which equipment rotates around a pivot and crops are watered with sprinklers;
	•	Travelling gun irrigation, in which a large-capacity, high-pressure nozzle throws water over crops as it is pulled through an alley in a field;
	•	Linear movement irrigation, in which a linear system of sprinklers supported by wheeled towers moves back and forth across a field, irrigating in a rectangular pattern. 
 - Drip irrigation: Also known as ‘microirrigation’ or trickle irrigation, examples include: 
	•	Surface drip irrigation, in which tubing or tape is placed on the surface of the soil, in a groove just below the soil surface.
	•	Subsurface drip irrigation, in which tubing or tape is buried in the soil at a depth below tillage equipment, making for highly permanent installations.   
- Smart/Precision irrigation Smart irrigation can include techniques and methods such as open- and closed-loop control, precision monitoring and control systems, and smart monitoring based on soil data, plant water status, weather data, and remote sensing.   
- Participatory irrigation management (PIM) Participatory irrigation management is the participation of irrigation users, mostly farmers at all levels and in all facets of irrigation management (such as planning, operation, maintenance, funding, regulations, monitoring, etc.), in irrigation. Elements include smart cards, prepaid metering systems and water user groups.   - Irrigation using harvested rainwater: Techniques include using barrels to collect rainwater and cisterns to store surface runoff from ditches, catch basins and rainwater gardens.   - Semi-circular bund: This is a form of in-situ rainwater harvesting that supports irrigation, by digging semi-lunar holes in the ground with the opening perpendicular to the flow of water; these slow down and retain runoff, ensuring that plants inside the semi-circles receive necessary water. They are particularly effective in arid and semi-arid regions.   - Rüza: Rüza is a traditional irrigation system, which involves impounding run-off water in ponds, to then use gravity-based irrigation. The water harvesting ponds are located at a higher elevation and are connected to the fields at the lower elevations through narrow drains.  - Hafir basins: Hafirs are shallow, excavated basins that collect and store rainwater for various uses, including irrigation. Further examples of water storage and reuse can be found in 1.1.2. Adaptation Measures (Water Storage, Distribution and Reuse).   - Mulching:  Mulching is the process of covering the topsoil with plant material such as leaves, grass, twigs, crop residues, straw, etc. Mulching can support infiltration of water after irrigation, by reducing evaporation and increasing water penetration into the soil. 
  3.3.3. Co-benefit of providing/supporting safe and clean water resources  As evidenced, specific types of irrigation systems and sustainable irrigation techniques can improve water efficiency and conserve water resources. These techniques could also help prevent contamination of surrounding freshwater bodies with fertilizers and other agricultural inputs by minimising over-irrigation – thereby reducing runoff – and by increasing soil absorption, which decreases stormwater runoff. In doing so, these systems offer the co-benefit of lowering infectious disease risks by supporting the continuous availability of safe and clean water resources.   Safe, potable water is vital for hygiene practices that help reduce infectious disease transmission. By conserving freshwater and preventing contamination of freshwater sources from runoff, certain irrigation technologies thus help maintain the availability of clean water for essential hygiene and public health, limiting the risk of infectious diseases.  
Research demonstrates that efficient irrigation water management, for instance, can substantially reduce water source contamination. A prime example of this is integrated drainage and wetland management, where irrigation, drainage, and wetland functions are combined into a single system. In this approach, the irrigated area is positioned above the wetland, and field drainage flows through pipes or ditches into the wetland, where nitrogen and phosphorus are biodegraded by plants. The treated water can then be reused for irrigation or discharged into nearby watercourses. Compared with conventional irrigation and drainage, studies report that total nitrogen concentrations at the wetland outlet were significantly reduced, compared to untreated field drainage water. As excessive nitrogen concentrations in surface and groundwater have been associated with cancers, reproductive problems, and other health risks, this underscores the importance of irrigation systems that help prevent agricultural contaminants from entering freshwater.   
3.3.4. Trade-off of ecological disruption
 Although there is debate over whether diverting freshwater for irrigation can ever be considered truly ecological, certain irrigation systems are clearly unsustainable, consuming large volumes of water and introducing agricultural pollutants into nearby surface and groundwater sources. In doing so, these systems undermine local ecosystems, posing the trade-off for infectious disease risk of ecological disruption.  Irrigation has been known to cause habitat and biodiversity loss, particularly in regions experiencing water stress. For example, agricultural irrigation often leads to salt runoff – salinising surface waters and threatening water-dependent species – as well as runoff of fertilisers and pesticides, which can cause eutrophication, bioaccumulation in edible fish species, and general ecological damage. Studies have shown that the accumulation of agrichemicals from irrigation runoff can weaken immune systems in fish, even at low concentrations, and that the delivery of irrigation water containing herbicides can cause aquatic plants to decompose, thereby potentially reducing oxygen levels to lethal levels for fish.  Additionally, increased soil salinisation can lead to plant withering, reduced growth, and loss of soil fertility, creating cascading consequences for the health of flora and fauna in local ecosystems. The abuse of groundwater sources for irrigation can potentially cause major habitat loss, simply by lowering water tables, as well as by leaching contaminants into groundwater. 
Moreover, even when irrigation systems consume a ‘sustainable’ quantity of water, their largely linear construction – linking agricultural territory to freshwater sources – can further disrupt ecosystems, often requiring the removal of trees, shrubs and other vegetation to make way for pipelines. Such practices fragment local habitats, reduce wildlife populations and impair ecosystem processes.   Finally, irrigation systems often rely on non-biodegradable plastics, such as tubing and pipes, that fragment through wear and environmental exposure, releasing microplastics into the soil. (Indeed, irrigation water was shown to be a primary source of microplastics in soil). These particles accumulate in agricultural ecosystems and can reduce soil moisture, slow plants’ development, and harm soil invertebrates.  Although the precise human health implications remain under study, emerging evidence shows that microplastics may spread pathogens and disrupt microbial communities, posing significant risks to ecosystem health.   By disrupting ecosystems, damaging habitats, and weakening biodiversity, these irrigation systems can directly affect infectious disease resilience and may increase the risk of disease emergence. This is because biodiverse and healthy ecosystems provide a range of habitats and promote genetic variability, which helps create natural checks on the spread of pathogens; variability in natural systems buffers against disease transmission by preventing the dominance of single disease vectors or hosts. When biodiversity is lost, this resilience weakens. In fact, ecosystem degradation and biodiversity loss have been identified as the primary environmental driver of infectious diseases. Consequently, irrigation systems that disrupt and compromise ecosystem health may inadvertently increase vulnerability to disease outbreaks.    For these reasons, irrigation systems must be designed with careful consideration of local ecological impacts to minimise habitat destruction and associated risks for infectious disease resilience. Research suggests that design priorities should include improving technological practices, incorporating natural water retention measures and selecting appropriate crops, adopting precision irrigation, and controlling agrochemical use. Additionally, system construction and operation should account for hydrogeological conditions, to prevent water depletion, reduce contamination risk, select appropriate irrigation systems, and protect drinking water sources. Further emphasis is also placed on biomonitoring for water quality control and contamination detection.
