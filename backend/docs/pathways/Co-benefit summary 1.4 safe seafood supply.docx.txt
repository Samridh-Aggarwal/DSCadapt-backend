Summary – Co-benefit 1.4: Safe seafood supply
1.4.1 Reduced human exposure to infectious disease
Annual per capita consumption of seafood in the EU was estimated at 20.96kg in 2024 and it projected to increase to 21.65kg by 2033. In 2022, 37.5% of consumed seafood in the EU was sourced locally, with the rest being covered by imports. According to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), seafood was identified as the vehicle of pathogens in 18.6% of recorded foodborne disease outbreaks between 2019 and 2023. Seafood was responsible for 5.6% of identified human cases, 8.9% of hospitalisations, and reported as a direct cause of 18.2% of related deaths in the EU over that period (24 deaths), making it the second largest factor in foodborne infectious disease mortality after meat. The recorded EFSA figures are likely to be a severe undercount, given that the WHO estimates 23 million foodborne infections in Europe annually, with an estimated 4700 deaths per year linked to foodborne diseases.
The provision of a safe seafood supply is therefore paramount in reducing human exposure to infectious diseases and can be an important co-benefit of climate adaptation measures in the coastal, marine, and fisheries sector, as well as in water management. This is of particular concern as climate change and the associated rise in sea temperatures is exacerbating the spread of critical pathogens of concern for human health in seafood, such as Vibrio bacteria. Other factors contributing to an increased exposure to seafood-borne infectious diseases are concentrated aquaculture, which facilitates the spread of pathogens among animals, and contaminated water, for example through sewage, agricultural runoff, or wastewater. The latter is particularly pronounced in molluscan shellfish which feed by filtering large amounts of water, thus bioconcentrating any pollutants present in their environment. This has led to viral concentrations in shellfish 100 times higher than in the surrounding seawater. Molluscs also tend to be ingested raw more often, which – together with undercooking – is a key transmission pathway of pathogens from animals to humans. 
Seafood can act as a vector for a number of viral, bacterial, and parasitic pathogens which are of concern for public health and human exposure to infectious disease in Europe. The most relevant are further explored below. 
Viruses
	•	Norovirus: Human noroviruses are a group of highly contagious Caliciviruses which are the most common cause for acute viral gastroenteritis, responsible for one third of cases in children and half in adults. They can be transmitted through contamination of food and water with faecal waste and are the most common cause of seafood-borne gastroenteritis, in particular via raw and undercooked shellfish. Studies have detected norovirus RNA in 9% of marketed oysters in France, 37% in Ireland, 52% of harvested bivalve shellfish in Italy and Spain, and 69% of oysters at the point-of-sale in the UK. The main source of shellfish contamination with norovirus stems from untreated sewage flowing into coastal waters, either through poor wastewater management, flood overflows, or overboard disposal of waste from boats. For example, several outbreak clusters of norovirus-linked gastroenteritis in France and Italy in 2002/03 were traced back to oysters harvested from the Etang de Thau in Southern France. Heavy rainfall had led to the overflow of a water purification station and subsequent faecal contamination of surrounding bodies of water. Consequently, climate adaptation measures that increase resilience against extreme precipitation or that manage wastewater, such as activated sludge and membrane bioreactors, offer significant co-benefits in reducing the prevalence of norovirus in seafood and therefore both hazard and human exposure to infectious disease. 
	•	Hepatitis A virus (HAV): Hepatitis A is a viral liver disease endemic to Europe. Among the key transmission pathways for HAV is the consumption of infected seafood. Similarly to noroviruses, this affects primarily molluscan shellfish harvested from waters contaminated with faecal waste. In fact, the largest viral food poisoning outbreak ever recorded occurred in 1988 in Shanghai, China, where over 300,000 cases of hepatitis A were linked to the consumption of clams. Smaller hepatitis A outbreaks linked to unsafe seafood have also been frequently recorded in Europe. Reducing pollution and spillage of wastewater into marine ecosystems thus offers a key pathway of preventing future outbreaks and transmission of HAV.
Bacteria
	•	Vibrio: Vibrios are a group of bacteria found in marine coastal and brackish waters that can cause Vibriosis. Vibrio spp. thrive in warmer waters with low salinity and are therefore highly climate sensitive. Consequently, Vibrio infections have been rising in Europe as warmer coastal water, especially in the Baltic and Black Seas, have expanded the suitable habitat for the bacteria. In 2022, 21 European countries possessed areas suitable for Vibrio spp. with a total population of 150 million people living along those coastal areas. On average over the past four decades, the length of Vibrio-suitable coastline in Europe has grown by 136km annually.  Unlike other bacteria like Salmonella and most viral seafood-borne pathogens which are introduced to marine environments through human or animal discharge, Vibrio spp. are endemically present in many aquatic ecosystems. Main transmission pathways for Vibrio bacteria are direct contact and ingestion through seafood. A recent EFSA study of European seafood found a prevalence of 19.6% for V. parahaemolyticus – one fifth of which with pathogenic strains –, 6.1% for V. vulnificus, and 4.1% for non-choleragenic V. cholerae, the three main species of concern in Europe. Besides exacerbating hazard and human exposure, the climate-induced spread of Vibrio spp. is also of growing concern for human vulnerability, as antimicrobial resistances are increasingly found in vibrios isolated from seafood. Establishing monitoring and surveillance systems for Vibrio is crucial in combatting its spread. Based on US-experience in surveillance, the ECDC has set up a Vibrio suitability tool with specific focus on the Baltic Sea.
	•	
	•	Salmonella: Non-typhoidal Salmonella are a group of zoonotic bacteria that cause salmonellosis, one of the most common food-borne diseases in Europe. The growth of Salmonella is highly susceptible to temperature and therefore both seasonal as well as climate sensitive. Research from across Europe estimates a 5-10% increase in salmonellosis cases for each one-degree increase in weekly ambient temperature. Transmission to humans usually occurs through ingestion of contaminated animal products, including through seafood. While contamination usually occurs through human or animal waste disposal into marine ecosystems and can thus be mitigated by improving wastewater management, sustainable aquacultural practices and adaptation measures that reduce stress and increase fish health have also shown to reduce the risk of Salmonella infections.
	•	Campylobacter: Campylobacter are a group of foodborne bacteria causing campylobacteriosis, and are considered the most common bacterial cause of human gastroenteritis worldwide. While mostly found in raw poultry, it also occurs in seafood and can be transmitted by consuming raw/undercooked shellfish. In Croatia, for example, Campylobacter spp. were found in 25.6% of bivalve molluscs samples. Similarly to Salmonella, Campylobacter cases are associated with higher temperatures and are thus both highly climate sensitive and seasonal in temperate climates. Consequently, disease burden is expected to increase in Europe, where the campylobacteriosis is already costing an estimated €2.4b annually. Infections via seafood are often linked to contamination of water sources in which filter-feeding molluscs accumulate the pathogens. Past outbreaks of Campylobacter have often been linked to heavy rain events, especially when preceded by droughts, as these can lead to manure runoff and sewage overflow.
	•	Listeria: L. monocytogenes is a pathogenic bacterium causing listeriosis. While less common than Salmonella and Campylobacter, it can lead to meningitis and sepsis, especially in immunocompromised people, and is considered among the most frequent causes of death due to foodborne diseases in Europe. In 2021, the EFSA reported a case-fatality rate for listeriosis of 13%, the highest of any zoonoses. Incidence of Listeria is observed to be increasing in industrialised countries worldwide. Unlike other bacteria it is highly temperature-tolerant and can even multiply just above freezing, making uninterrupted cooling chains an insufficient preventative measure. It also exhibits a degree of heat resistance and has been shown to survive in shrimp boiled up to five minutes. L. monocytogenes occurs naturally in terrestrial and aquatic environments and is frequently found in raw foods extracted from these environments, in particular seafood. A recent study from Northern Greece isolated the bacterium from 11.5% of fish samples. Therefore, contamination pathways for seafood are highly complex and can occur both environmentally and during handling and processing. In addition, AMR is a growing concern for L. monocytogenes. 
	•	Escherichia coli: Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) are the fourth most reported foodborne gastrointestinal infection in humans in the EU. Transmission of STEC occurs through faecal contamination and the bacteria have been detected in both shell- and finfish worldwide. A 2015/16 study of oysters at the point of sale in the UK found that 2.4% of samples exceeded EU legal standards. Sewage spillage has also led to the closure of shellfish production sites in the UK, bearing significant livelihood impacts for producers. Similar to other pathogenic bacteria which thrive in warmer temperatures, STEC are highly seasonal and are therefore of growing concern for foodborne infectious disease in Europe due to climate change-induced warming. An increasing number of studies have also detected antimicrobial resistance in STEC strains found in seafood, for example in the USA, Canada, and South Korea.
