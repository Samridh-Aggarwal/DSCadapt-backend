Potentially best placed in another pathway  Wastewater infrastructure is the complex network of pipes, stations and treatment facilities that collect, transport, treat and dispose of sewage and other wastewater from homes, businesses, and industries. Wastewater management is essential for maintaining a functioning, healthy society, and indispensable for production activities and sanitation safety.   1.2.1. Intended outcome as an adaptation to climate change  Climate change presents a serious threat to wastewater infrastructures; extreme weather and hazards exacerbated by climate change can disrupt the services and treatment efficiency of wastewater infrastructure, placing sewer systems at risk of overflow, breakage, and corrosion.  For example: heavy rainstorms can overload sewerage systems, causing sewer clogging and breakage and reduce the capacity of treatment facilities; flooding from rivers, lakes and sea can overwhelm drainage systems, leading to sewage overspills; rising temperatures can hamper the efficiency of treatment processes; drought-produced low flows that hold higher concentrations of antibiotic resistance genes, pollutants and pharmaceuticals place increased pressures on wastewater treatment processes.  These can lead to the release of untreated, insufficiently treated, or contaminated water into the water network and the natural environment. Both stormwater overflows, and insufficiently treated wastewater, due to the presence of sewage, contain waste, pollutants, and contaminants, including diverse intestinal and soil-inhabiting bacteria, posing significant risks to public health.  Wastewater management is therefore a critical adaptation strategy to increase the resilience of wastewater infrastructure to the impacts of climate change, while supporting a water network that enables hygienic sanitation practices.
Effective wastewater management can reduce risks to public health associated with contaminated water and damaged sewage systems, while also improving the efficiency and reliability of public services in protecting the population.   1.2.2. Adaptation Measures  The most appropriate types of wastewater adaptation measures will depend on the specific characteristics of the water infrastructure, sewer or wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) in question. Factors such as whether the context is urban or rural, the income level of the region, the type of waste treated (chemical, hazardous, animal waste, etc.), and climate patterns significantly influence which measures are most critical for enhancing the resilience of local wastewater infrastructure. To maximise the effectiveness of these improvements, risk analysis is considered an essential tool for evaluating critical infrastructure,  helping identify and evaluate vulnerabilities, interdependencies, and potential gaps. Understandably, wastewater infrastructure falls under the European Union’s Directive on the Resilience of Critical Entities, which stipulates that risk analysis be conducted every four years.    Examples:   - Regular dredging of sewer pipes and septic tanks - Building septic tanks with large capacity  - Retrofitting and expanding WWTP  - Early Warning Systems:  Monitoring, modelling and simulation tools that can help predict and mitigate failures in wastewater infrastructure  - Automation & Advanced Process Control - Addition of retention basins to WWTPs: A retention basin at a wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) is a type of stormwater management facility that temporarily stores stormwater runoff, in order to meter it out to a WWTP. This can prevent overloading of the treatment plant and improves water quality.  before the water is released to the treatment plant or eventually flows into the environment.   - Addition of sewers and increase of sewer size to handle increased rainfall   - Sewer construction with anti-corrosion coating to withstand decreased rainfall - Extending hydraulic retention times and improving aeration strength in WWTPs to handle higher contaminant load in influent water in periods of decreased rainfall  - Treating combined sewer overflows (CSO): Treating CSOs before discharge in the receiving water body (e.g. by constructed wetlands) was evaluated to be more cost-effective than preventing CSOs.
- Combined sewerage systems Combined sewerage systems integrate wastewater from homes and businesses with stormwater from streets and drains into one pipeline. Combined sewerage systems offer both advantages and disadvantages as an adaptation measure. On some occasions combined systems will overflow into the water environment – they are designed to do so during significant rain to prevent the risk of flooding to people and property and protect the functioning of WWTPs. However, some overflows may cause unacceptable environmental damage that must be addressed.  - Decentralized wastewater treatment  The collection and treatment of wastewater or fractions of it (e.g. black water, greywater, rainwater) at or near the point of generation using a range of treatment technologies and methods allowing for safe disposal and/or fit-for purpose water reuse at a local level. This can reduce the pressure on centralized wastewater infrastructure during extreme weather.
- Disconnection of surface water from combined sewer systems: This is a measure that involves the control of surface water to the sewer system, to reduce or prevent a combination of flooding and/ or combined sewer overflow (CSO) spills and improve receiving water qualities.  - Emergency Response Planning, e.g., installing emergency generators, floodproofing critical equipment    1.2.3. Co-benefit of reducing exposure to flood water  Upgrading wastewater infrastructure through effective risk management, resilience planning, and adaptation measures helps it withstand sudden and intense stormwater surges, preventing sewer overflows and limiting the release of floodwater. This reduces human exposure to contaminated water, lowering the risk of contact with infectious pathogens and the spread of waterborne illnesses, such as E. coli, shigellosis (known historically as dysentery), and cryptosporidiosis.   Floodwaters can also elevate the risk of vector-borne diseases; areas of standing or stagnant water create ideal breeding conditions for mosquitoes, thus increasing the risk of mosquito-borne diseases such as malaria, dengue fever, and West Nile virus. Reducing exposure to floodwater therefore has the benefit of reducing the risk of both waterborne and mosquito-borne infections.   1.2.4. Co-benefit of contributing to healthy ecosystems  
Effective wastewater management and treatment can prevent the release of untreated, insufficiently treated, or contaminated water into the water network and the natural environment. Higher levels of contaminants – such as PFAS, fertilisers, microplastics and pathogens – in wastewater, sewage, and floodwaters threaten to disrupt ecosystems, weakening biodiversity and ecological balance.   For example, microplastics can cause bioaccumulation in the food chain, disrupting ecological balance and provoking a loss of biodiversity. Chemicals and nutrients in fertilisers can cause algal blooms in aquatic ecosystems, reducing oxygen content in water and threatening populations of aquatic species. Sewage pollution of freshwater ecosystems is associated with an increase in the concentration and transmission of human pathogens, as well as the incidence of heavy metal toxicity in humans and fish. Contaminated waters can degrade soil quality, triggering a consequential reduction in biodiversity among both plant and animal species and negative impacts on local ecosystems. Poorly treated effluent from WWTPs has also been evidenced to reduce microbial diversity in waters, and by consequence, poses a risk of increasing anti-microbial and antibiotic resistance.   Preventing ecosystem contamination is therefore essential for maintaining ecosystem health, which can support biodiversity and ecological balance, potentially limiting the prevalence of disease vectors. Upgrading wastewater infrastructure thus offers the added benefit of contributing to healthier ecosystems and enhancing resilience to infectious diseases.  
1.2.5. Co-benefit of providing safe seafood supply
 Poor wastewater management is a key source of pollution for coastal and marine ecosystems. Sewage that spills into these environments inevitably contaminates the organisms living within them and affects not least seafood, the vast majority of which is caught or harvested from these ecosystems. This carries significant risk of seafood-borne infections for humans, especially when the seafood is consumed raw or undercooked. Transmission of many of the most significant foodborne pathogens in Europe, such as noroviruses, hepatitis A virus, Salmonella spp., Campylobacter spp., and E. coli occurs through the faecal-oral route. Bivalve molluscs in particular accumulate pathogens and toxins from their wastewater-contaminated environment through filter feeding. Climate adaptation measures that improve wastewater management and reduce the spillage of sewage into coastal waters therefore carry significant co-benefits for increasing the safety of seafood for human consumption.
